\vspace{2em}

\noindent Queue management in Philippine higher education institutions
remains predominantly manual, resulting in inefficient counter
assignments, inconsistent service monitoring, and prolonged student
wait times during peak periods. This paper presents QueueFlow, a Smart
Queue Management System that uses You Only Look Once version~8 nano
(YOLOv8n) with object tracking, OpenCV camera processing, automated
queue-number assignment, ticket generation, and real-time wait-time
prediction. The implemented prototype separates the detector from the
web backend: a camera-connected detector process performs YOLO
inference, filters duplicate and implausible detections, estimates
crowd metrics, and pushes tracked-person metadata to a FastAPI backend.
The backend confirms new queue entrants through a configurable queue
zone, candidate confirmation window, motion checks, track-ID remapping,
and hybrid appearance-spatial re-identification before issuing a queue
number. The intended deployment issues physical queue tickets through a
thermal printer; for prototype demonstration, the system temporarily
generates PDF tickets containing the same queue number, QR code, short
code, and JSON Web Token (JWT). Ticket records are stored in MySQL, and
students can check live status through a mobile browser or mobile
tracker. The staff dashboard displays live annotated snapshots, queue
state, no-show alerts, counter controls, analytics, and forecasts.
Prediction outputs include current estimated wait time and 5, 15, and
30~min forecasts using an M/M/c wait-time baseline, short-term trend
projection, Holt's double exponential smoothing, and a mean-reversion
long forecast. Developed for Naga College Foundation, Inc.\ (NCF) in
Naga City, Philippines, QueueFlow is evaluated in terms of queue
assignment accuracy, re-identification reliability, ticket and token
validation, prediction accuracy using MAE and MAPE, system latency, and
user satisfaction.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background of the Study}

Campus service delivery depends not only on the availability of staff
and counters, but also on how quickly an office can recognize demand as
it forms. During enrollment, payment, clearance, and other academic
transactions, students often gather faster than service personnel can
manually count, organize, and update the queue. This creates a practical
problem for both sides of the service process: students have limited
visibility of their waiting status, while staff have limited real-time
information for deciding when to serve, skip, recall, or prepare
additional service capacity.

At Naga College Foundation Inc.\ (NCF), queue congestion is not simply
a matter of long lines. It affects the continuity of student
transactions because campus offices such as enrollment, cashiering, and
library services are part of a connected workflow. A delay in one
service point can influence the next step a student needs to complete.
The Commission on Higher Education recognizes accessibility and
efficient service delivery as important indicators of institutional
quality~\cite{ched2022}; therefore, improving queue visibility is also
part of improving the overall student service experience.

Conventional queue systems help arrange people in order, but they
usually begin only after a student manually asks for a number or after
staff encode the transaction. These systems can record issued tickets,
yet they do not automatically observe the actual service area, confirm
new arrivals from a camera view, prevent duplicate queue entries, or
estimate how the line may change in the next few minutes. Earlier queue
management tools also tend to focus on static numbering rather than
combining automated detection, staff monitoring, student status access,
and prediction in one workflow~\cite{shah2018,chen2023adaptive}.

QueueFlow is proposed in this study as a computer vision based queue
management prototype for campus service offices. Instead of relying
only on manual queue entry, the system uses a camera-connected
YOLOv8n and OpenCV pipeline to detect persons inside a defined queue
zone. YOLO is suitable for this task because it performs real-time
object detection in a single network pass~\cite{redmon2018}, and
YOLOv8n provides a lightweight model that can be used in practical
prototype settings~\cite{jocher2022}. QueueFlow extends detection with
duplicate filtering, candidate confirmation, and hybrid re-entry
matching so that queue records remain more consistent when people move
or briefly leave the camera frame.

The queue ticket component of QueueFlow is designed for thermal printer
deployment, allowing a physical queue ticket to be issued after a queue
number is assigned. Because the prototype is demonstrated without the
final printer hardware, the current implementation generates a PDF
ticket as a temporary substitute. This PDF carries the same operational
information expected from the thermal ticket, including the queue
number, short code, and QR link for student status checking.

Beyond ticket generation, QueueFlow connects the detector, queue
records, staff dashboard, and prediction module through a FastAPI-based
backend and MySQL database. The prediction module supports current,
5-minute, 15-minute, and 30-minute wait-time estimates using queuing
logic and short-term forecasting. Through this design, the study aims
to provide NCF with a prototype that improves queue visibility,
supports staff decision-making, and demonstrates how computer vision
and predictive analytics can be applied to campus service management in
a Philippine educational institution.

The Review of Related Literature therefore examines the technologies
and concepts that support each part of the problem identified above.
Computer vision and YOLO literature address automated queue
observation, queue management literature explains ticketing and
identity maintenance, and forecasting literature supports the
wait-time estimates needed for staff decision-making.

\section{Review of Related Literature}

This section reviews related studies according to the major needs
identified in the Background of the Study. First, it discusses computer
vision for recognizing people in service areas. Second, it reviews YOLO
as the detection approach used by the prototype. Third, it examines
queue management systems, especially the limitations of manual and
static ticketing. Finally, it presents predictive analytics as the
basis for transforming live queue records into current and near-future
wait-time estimates.

\textbf{Computer Vision for Real-Time Person Detection.}
Computer vision has emerged as a transformative technology for
automated crowd monitoring in institutional settings, enabling
non-intrusive, real-time crowd density estimation and behavioral
pattern recognition. Modern deep learning approaches process video
streams to extract meaningful crowd metrics including density maps,
movement trajectories, and occupancy levels across designated zones
without compromising individual privacy~\cite{zhang2023}. Kumar
et~al.~\cite{kumar2022} demonstrated that deep learning-based crowd
monitoring systems achieve superior accuracy even when handling
occlusions and varying lighting conditions common in institutional
environments. Park et~al.~\cite{park2023} explored lightweight model
architectures achieving processing speeds of 25--30~fps on embedded
GPU platforms. Privacy-preserving techniques---including pose
estimation without facial recognition, immediate anonymization at the
camera level, and encrypted transmission of aggregate
statistics---are recommended for deployment in public-facing
institutional service areas~\cite{bergman2023}. These principles
directly inform QueueFlow's design, which processes bounding box data
and colour histograms without storing or transmitting raw video.

\textbf{You Only Look Once (YOLO).}
YOLO represents a breakthrough in real-time object detection by
processing entire images in a single forward pass through the neural
network. Unlike conventional detection methods that rely on region
proposals or sliding windows, YOLO's unified architecture
simultaneously predicts bounding boxes and class probabilities,
enabling systems to detect and count people with exceptional speed and
accuracy~\cite{redmon2018}. The objectness score for each detection is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Confidence}(\text{box}) = \Pr(\text{Object}) \times
  \text{IoU}(\text{pred},\,\text{truth})
  \label{eq:yolo_conf}
\end{equation}
where $\Pr(\text{Object})$ is the probability that an object exists in
the cell and IoU represents the intersection over union between
predicted and ground truth boxes~\cite{jocher2022}.

\textbf{Queue Management Systems.}
Queue management systems in institutional settings have evolved from
physical ticket dispensers and manual counting to digital platforms
with varying degrees of automation. Camera-based queue monitoring
approaches offer a fundamentally different model by passively detecting
and tracking all persons who enter a service area, eliminating the
manual entry requirement entirely~\cite{alahmari2021}.
Re-identification of returning persons is a technically challenging
aspect of camera-based systems requiring robust person tracking beyond
simple frame-to-frame bounding box matching~\cite{rodriguez2024}.

\textbf{Predictive Analytics for Service Demand Forecasting.}
Predictive analytics transforms queue management from reactive to
proactive by forecasting demand patterns and identifying potential
bottlenecks before they materialize. The M/M/c queuing model, as
established in the foundational queueing theory literature, enables
calculation of key performance metrics including average wait times,
queue lengths, and system utilization~\cite{shortle2018fundamentals}. System
utilization is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \frac{\lambda}{c\mu}
  \label{eq:utilization}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the arrival rate, $\mu$ the service rate, and $c$
the number of active servers. The M/M/c model processes real-time
arrival rates from computer vision systems to calculate immediate wait
times using Little's Law:
\begin{equation}
  W = \frac{L}{\lambda}
  \label{eq:littles_law}
\end{equation}
For medium-term forecasting, Holt's double exponential smoothing tracks
both queue level and trend with separate smoothing parameters~\cite{holt2004forecasting}.
For the 30~min forecast exposed by the prototype, growth-ratio and
mean-reversion logic compare early versus recent queue history to
detect systematic demand changes.

\section{Synthesis of the State-of-the-Art}

The literature reviewed in this chapter supports the central concern
identified in the Background of the Study: campus service offices need
better real-time visibility of queue conditions, not merely a list of
issued numbers. Studies on computer vision show that camera-based
person detection can reduce dependence on manual observation by
capturing activity in a service area as it happens. YOLO-based
approaches are especially relevant because they provide the speed
needed for live monitoring, making them suitable for a prototype that
must observe students entering and moving through a defined queue zone.

However, the reviewed studies also show that detection is only the
first layer of a complete queue management system. A person detector
can identify bounding boxes, but it does not automatically solve the
operational problems described in the Background: duplicate entries,
temporary disappearance from the camera view, skipped queue positions,
or student access to queue status. This makes tracking, deduplication,
candidate confirmation, and re-entry matching necessary additions when
computer vision is applied to actual service-office queues.

Research on queue management and predictive analytics further
reinforces the need to move beyond static ticket numbering. Traditional
queue tools can arrange students in order, but they provide limited
support for staff decisions when demand changes quickly. Queuing theory
and short-term forecasting address this limitation by translating
arrival rates, active counters, and queue length into wait-time
estimates. Multi-horizon prediction is therefore useful because staff
need both immediate queue status and near-future indicators for
counter planning.

Taken together, the reviewed works suggest that an effective campus
queue solution should combine four functions in one workflow:
camera-based queue entry, reliable queue identity management, ticket
and status access for students, and predictive information for staff.
These findings connect directly to QueueFlow's design, which integrates
YOLOv8n detection, hybrid re-entry matching, thermal-ticket-oriented
queue number generation with a PDF demonstration fallback, a staff
dashboard, and current, 5-minute, 15-minute, and 30-minute wait-time
estimates.

Despite these advances, existing studies remain limited when viewed
against the specific needs of NCF's campus service context. Many focus
on crowd counting, general queue applications, or prediction models as
separate components. Fewer works demonstrate how these components can
operate together in a Philippine educational institution where queue
visibility, staff action, student status checking, and practical
deployment constraints must be handled in a single prototype.

\section{Gap Bridged by the Study}

This study addresses a specific practical gap: there is currently no
integrated system that combines passive camera-based queue entry using
YOLO, hybrid multi-tier person re-identification, and multi-algorithm
multi-horizon wait time prediction, specifically designed for campus
service delivery in a Philippine educational institution context.

QueueFlow responds to this gap by developing these components in a
unified architecture: a YOLO-based person detection and tracking
pipeline with a custom NMS pre-filter and post-frame deduplication
layer, a hybrid re-entry matching algorithm that combines spatial
proximity and appearance similarity, thermal-ticket-oriented queue
number generation with a PDF demonstration fallback, and prediction
models exposed as current, 5, 15, and 30~min wait-time estimates.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

The theoretical framework of this study is anchored on the queue
visibility problem described in the Background of the Study. Campus
service offices need a way to observe student arrival, maintain queue
order, estimate waiting time, and present useful information to both
staff and students. QueueFlow addresses this problem through the
combined application of computer vision, queuing theory, and predictive
analytics.

\emph{Computer Vision Theory} provides the basis for replacing manual
line observation with automated person detection. In QueueFlow, this
theory is applied through YOLOv8n and OpenCV, where camera frames are
processed to detect persons inside a defined queue zone. YOLO's
single-pass detection approach supports real-time monitoring because it
can identify objects quickly within each frame~\cite{redmon2018}.
For the study, computer vision supplies the first layer of queue
visibility: it allows the system to recognize possible queue entrants
without requiring every student to manually register at a counter.

\emph{Queuing Theory}, as formalized in classical queueing
literature~\cite{shortle2018fundamentals}, explains how arrivals,
service capacity, and waiting lines behave in a service system. The
M/M/c model represents a multi-counter service environment in which
the arrival rate, service rate, and number of active counters influence
queue length, utilization, and waiting time. Together with Little's
Law, this theory provides the mathematical basis for estimating waiting
time, queue length, and counter utilization from real-time arrival and
service rates. In QueueFlow, queuing theory is connected to the system
through queue records, active counter settings, and service-time
assumptions. Once the computer vision layer identifies queue activity,
queuing theory provides the formulas for estimating how long students
may wait and how staff decisions about counters may affect service
flow.

\emph{Predictive Analytics and Forecasting Theory} extends the system
from observing the present queue to anticipating near-future demand.
The Background of the Study emphasizes that queue conditions change
throughout the day, especially during enrollment and payment periods.
For this reason, QueueFlow uses short-term forecasting with queuing
logic, trend projection, smoothing, and mean-reversion principles to
produce current, 5-minute, 15-minute, and 30-minute estimates. These
forecasts support staff decision-making by giving the dashboard more
than a static queue count; they provide an early indication of whether
congestion is likely to increase or decrease~\cite{brown2022,holt2004forecasting}.

Together, these theories form the foundation of QueueFlow's design.
Computer vision converts camera input into observable queue activity;
queuing theory converts queue activity and counter capacity into
service metrics; and predictive analytics converts those metrics into
near-future wait-time estimates. The resulting information is delivered
through the staff dashboard, student status access, and queue ticket
workflow, where the intended deployment uses a thermal printer and the
current prototype uses PDF tickets as a demonstration fallback.

\section{Conceptual Framework}

The conceptual framework adopts the Input-Process-Output-Outcome
(IPOO) model to illustrate how raw camera data is transformed through
the QueueFlow pipeline into actionable queue management intelligence.
The \emph{Input} consists of a live camera feed, queue-zone settings,
counter configuration, service-time assumptions, and staff actions.
The \emph{Process} involves YOLOv8n detection, NMS pre-filtering,
zone-based filtering, hybrid re-entry matching, candidate accumulation
over a minimum confirmation window, queue state management including
no-show detection and position recalculation, ticket generation, and
multi-horizon prediction. The \emph{Output} delivers a real-time queue
list with assigned numbers, ticket credentials, individual wait times,
and prediction cards to both the staff web dashboard and the student
mobile portal. The \emph{Outcome} is improved queue visibility,
reduced manual ticket handling, and better staff decision support.

\section{Statement of the Problem}

This study seeks to address the following problems:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What are the current limitations of the manual queue management
        process at NCF service offices during peak demand periods?
  \item How can a YOLO-based passive detection pipeline with hybrid
        re-identification reliably assign and maintain queue numbers
        without student interaction?
  \item To what degree of accuracy can the implemented prediction
        models forecast current, 5~min, 15~min, and 30~min wait times?
  \item How do service staff and administrators evaluate QueueFlow in
        terms of usability, detection reliability, ticket generation,
        and prediction usefulness?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Assumptions}

Several assumptions underlie the proposed study. First, that the camera
hardware provides sufficient video quality and coverage of the queue
zone to enable reliable YOLO person detection under the lighting
conditions and pedestrian densities typical of NCF service areas.
Second, that YOLO's tracking mechanism, supplemented by the system's
spatial re-identification layer, produces sufficiently stable person
identities across the queue duration. Third, that the three prediction
models generate forecasts that are actionable and sufficiently accurate
for staff scheduling and counter management decisions. Fourth, that the
thermal printer can be configured in the deployment area; where printer
hardware is unavailable, PDF ticket generation can demonstrate the same
queue-number, QR-code, and token workflow. Fifth, that service staff and
administrators possess basic digital literacy to operate the web
dashboard and interpret the system's outputs.

\section{Scope and Delimitation}

This study focuses on the design, implementation, and evaluation of
QueueFlow at NCF in Naga City, Philippines, covering the development
of a YOLOv8n-based detection pipeline, a hybrid re-entry matching
algorithm, a multi-horizon prediction engine, a FastAPI-based REST~API,
a MySQL-backed data layer, and a ticket generation module designed for
thermal-printer deployment. The current demonstration build generates
PDF tickets with QR codes as a temporary substitute for printed thermal
tickets. The system is deployed as a single-queue-zone, single-service
instance. It does not currently support simultaneous multi-zone or
multi-department management, priority queue differentiation based on
student status or urgency factors, direct integration with the Campus
Management System or Student Information System, or IoT sensor
integration beyond the camera feed and queue ticket printer. The
evaluation period is restricted to one semester of post-implementation
operation.

\section{Significance of the Study}

The most immediate beneficiaries of this research are the service staff
and students of NCF. Service staff gain a real-time queue dashboard
that eliminates manual counting and provides data-driven insight into
current queue length, individual wait times, and demand forecasts
across three time horizons---enabling proactive counter management
rather than reactive response to visible crowding. Students benefit
from a passive system that requires no action on their part: upon
entering the monitored area, a queue number is assigned and immediately
accessible through any mobile browser using their access token.

For institutional administration, QueueFlow provides queue performance
records that support evidence-based decisions about staffing, service
hours, and physical layout. For the academic community, the study
advances the integration of computer vision, person re-identification,
ticket generation, and multi-algorithm predictive analytics in service
queue management.

\section{Definition of Terms}

\textbf{Access Token.} A ticket credential assigned to each confirmed
queue number. In the implemented prototype, this consists of a short
code and a JWT-backed validation record that allows the student to
check queue status without a login account.

\textbf{Candidate Accumulation.} A buffer mechanism requiring a new
detection to appear consistently for a minimum confirmation window
before a queue number is assigned, preventing transient detections from
generating spurious tickets. The prototype uses a configurable default
of 14 confirmation frames.

\textbf{Holt's Double Exponential Smoothing.} A forecasting method
based on exponentially weighted moving averages~\cite{holt2004forecasting}
used for the 15~min prediction, applying separate smoothing parameters
for queue level ($\alpha = 0.4$) and trend ($\beta = 0.2$) with damping
factor 0.85 to avoid over-projection.

\textbf{M/M/c Model.} A foundational multi-server queueing model from
classical queueing theory~\cite{shortle2018fundamentals} used for
short-term (5--15~min) wait time prediction, calculating
$W = L/\lambda$ from Little's Law using real-time arrival rates from
the YOLO detection pipeline.

\textbf{Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS).} Both a standard IoU-based NMS
pass and a custom X-column/Y-adjacency filter applied to raw YOLO
outputs before they reach the queue tracker, suppressing duplicate head
and body boxes YOLO commonly emits for a single person.

\textbf{Queue Zone.} A configurable rectangular region defined by four
pixel coordinates on the camera frame; only persons whose bounding box
centre falls within this region are tracked and assigned queue numbers.

\textbf{Thermal Queue Ticket.} The intended physical output of the
system containing the queue number, QR code, and short code. In the
prototype demonstration, the same ticket information is generated as a
PDF while thermal printer hardware is not yet attached.

\textbf{Re-Entry Matching.} A hybrid algorithm that first matches a
new detection to a missing person by spatial proximity, then by
appearance histogram similarity, and finally by recency as a last
resort.

\textbf{You Only Look Once (YOLO).} The YOLOv8n model with
\texttt{persist=True} tracking, configurable input size, and confidence
threshold, used to detect and track persons entering the queue zone in
camera frames.

\chapter{Review of Related Literature}

\section{Computer Vision for Real-Time Person Detection}

The application of computer vision to person detection and crowd
monitoring in institutional settings has matured significantly over the
past decade. Modern deep learning architectures process video streams
to extract crowd metrics including density maps, movement trajectories,
and occupancy levels without compromising individual
privacy~\cite{zhang2023}. Park et~al.~\cite{park2023} demonstrated
that lightweight model architectures optimized for edge devices achieve
25--30~fps on embedded GPU platforms, making real-time crowd analytics
feasible within the budget constraints of educational institutions.
Beyond simple counting, modern systems track individual trajectories
through service areas, identifying dwell times at service counters and
queue formation patterns~\cite{nguyen2023}.

Privacy-preserving techniques such as pose estimation without facial
recognition, camera-level anonymization, and transmission of aggregate
statistics address ethical concerns inherent in camera-based monitoring
in educational environments~\cite{bergman2023}.

\section{YOLO-Based Tracking and Deduplication}

YOLO's single-pass detection architecture delivers the speed necessary
for real-time queue monitoring, processing entire frames in one forward
pass to simultaneously predict bounding boxes, confidence scores, and
class labels~\cite{redmon2018}. The YOLOv8 series advances this
through improved multi-scale feature pyramid networks and anchor-free
detection heads, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy-speed
tradeoffs~\cite{jocher2022}.

The duplicate detection problem---where YOLO emits multiple bounding
boxes for a single person---is particularly pronounced in queue
environments where people face the camera at close range. Custom
geometric tests using X-column overlap and Y-adjacency thresholds have
been proposed as supplements to standard NMS~\cite{rodriguez2024},
forming the basis of the pre-filter implemented in QueueFlow's
detection pipeline.

\section{Queue Management and Re-Identification}

Camera-based queue monitoring enables passive, continuous tracking of
service user arrival order without requiring any student
interaction~\cite{alahmari2021}. The core challenge beyond detection is
person re-identification: when a tracked individual temporarily
disappears from view and reappears with a new track ID, the system must
recognize them as the same person and restore their original queue
position rather than assigning a new number~\cite{rodriguez2024}.

Re-identification approaches fall into three categories:
appearance-based methods using colour histograms or deep feature
embeddings, spatial-temporal methods using last known position and time
elapsed, and hybrid methods combining both signals~\cite{chen2023mobile}.

\section{Predictive Analytics for Service Demand Forecasting}

Multi-horizon forecasting provides both immediate and strategic
insights by generating predictions at different time scales, each
matched to a specific operational decision type~\cite{brown2022}. The
M/M/c queuing model provides analytical wait time calculations from
real-time arrival and service rate measurements, making it suitable for
current wait-time estimates and near-term tactical
predictions~\cite{shortle2018fundamentals}.

For the 15~min range, Holt's double exponential
smoothing~\cite{holt2004forecasting} extends simple exponential
smoothing by separately tracking queue level and trend. Damping the
trend component prevents over-projection during transient spikes, a
practical necessity in campus service environments where queue lengths
can surge and dissipate quickly. For the 30~min forecast implemented in
QueueFlow, mean-reversion logic is used to limit long forecast
uncertainty.

\chapter{Methodology}

\section{Research Design}

This study utilized a Mixed-Methods and Developmental research design
to systematically develop and evaluate QueueFlow. Quantitative data
focused on technical performance metrics such as queue number
assignment accuracy, re-entry matching accuracy, prediction model MAE
and MAPE across three forecasting horizons, and real-time processing
latency. Qualitative data was gathered through semi-structured
interviews exploring the experiences and perceptions of service staff
and administrators.

\section{Population and Locale}

The study was conducted at Naga College Foundation, Inc.\ (NCF)
located in Naga City, Camarines Sur, Philippines. NCF was selected as
the research site because it serves large volumes of student service
transactions across enrollment, cashiering, and library operations,
with pronounced peak periods that create the queue management
challenges QueueFlow is designed to address.

\section{Research Instrument}

The study utilized a researcher-made structured questionnaire employing
a five-point Likert scale to measure respondents' level of agreement
with statements related to system usability, detection reliability,

\section{Ethical Considerations}

This study strictly adheres to ethical standards to protect
participants' rights, ensure data privacy, and maintain research
integrity. Participation was entirely voluntary and informed consent
was obtained from all participants. All personal information was
anonymized and stored in password-protected systems. The study complied
with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No.~10173).

\section{Statistical Treatment of Data}

Data gathered was processed using both descriptive and technical
performance metrics. Descriptive analysis used the Weighted Mean
($\text{WM} = \Sigma(f \times w)/N$), Overall Weighted Mean, and
Percentage to summarize survey responses. System performance was
evaluated using:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MAE} = \frac{\sum |y_{\text{predicted}} - y_{\text{actual}}|}{n}
  \label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum\left|
    \frac{y_{\text{actual}} - y_{\text{predicted}}}{y_{\text{actual}}}
  \right| \times 100\%
  \label{eq:mape}
\end{equation}
calculated separately for each of the three prediction model horizons.

\section{System Design and Architecture}

QueueFlow is implemented as a FastAPI-based web application with a
separate camera detector process, a MySQL backend, staff dashboard
pages, and public queue-status endpoints for students as shown in
Table~\ref{tab:tools}. The detector process captures frames through
OpenCV \texttt{VideoCapture}, runs YOLOv8n tracking with configurable
image size and confidence threshold, filters duplicate and implausible
bounding boxes, extracts crowd and prediction metrics, and sends
tracked-person metadata to the backend. The backend then performs
queue-zone filtering, candidate confirmation, track-ID remapping,
re-entry matching, queue-state management, and ticket generation. The
intended ticket output is a thermal queue ticket; the demonstration
build uses generated PDF tickets with QR codes as a temporary
substitute.

\section{Software Development Process}

The system was developed iteratively using the Agile development
methodology, organized into sprint-based cycles covering: YOLO pipeline
setup and NMS pre-filter development; re-entry matching algorithm
design and debugging; no-show detection implementation; prediction
model development and accuracy tuning; FastAPI REST~API and staff
dashboard development; thermal-ticket workflow design with temporary
PDF ticket generation for demonstration; and student queue-status
integration testing.

\section{Validation and Testing Methods}

System validation was conducted through four primary approaches.
\emph{Alpha Testing} was conducted internally by the developers to
identify and resolve functional issues. \emph{Beta Testing} released
the system to a limited group of service staff at NCF to observe
real-world interaction and collect feedback. \emph{Usability
Evaluation} employed the structured Likert-scale questionnaire
administered to service staff and administrators after a minimum of
two weeks of regular system use. \emph{Performance Testing} evaluated
processing latency, prediction accuracy using MAE and MAPE against
ground-truth wait times, re-entry matching accuracy verified against
direct observation logs, and ticket flow validation from queue
assignment to QR/short-code status lookup.
