\vspace{1em}

\noindent At the cashiering office of Naga College Foundation, Inc.\
(NCF), as in many campus service offices, students cannot see how many
people are ahead of them or how long they will wait. \sysname{} was
built as a capstone prototype to close that gap. A YOLOv8n detector
with ByteTrack tracks people in a calibrated queue zone, and students
who have enrolled a face through the mobile application and asked to
be queued are identified with ArcFace face embeddings. A match is
accepted only if its similarity reaches 0.30 and exceeds that of the
second-best candidate by a margin of 0.15; a face that fails either test
is referred to staff rather than guessed. An accepted student is issued
a queue number and ticket without manual input and follows their
position and estimated wait on the mobile application, while staff
manage the queue from a web dashboard. A baseline survey of 83 students
and 7 staff found that students strongly agreed that the current process
leaves them without information (overall weighted mean 4.64), while
staff were neutral (3.34). On 45
held-out photographs of 17 identities, the decision rule linked 97.8\%
to the correct student and none to a wrong one, and it refused all 45
non-enrolled probes. All seven numbers issued during testing were
minted without manual input, and the backend answered 2,536 requests
from 50 concurrent users with no failures. On 166 observed cashier
transactions, a predictor based on the number of people ahead reached a
mean absolute error of 1.85 minutes, against 2.07 minutes for a constant
baseline. Twenty students who used the system in a supervised trial
rated it Strongly Agree on usability, reliability, and clarity (overall
weighted mean 4.32), and eight cashier staff rated it Agree (3.86),
lowest on reliability. In a user acceptance test of the mobile
application, held without the queue-zone detector and on a poor
connection, 42 students rated its steps Agree (4.13), and 15 of 18 face
registrations were saved. The results show that face recognition with a
margin requirement can issue queue numbers without manual input while
refusing uncertain matches. They come from offline tests and short
supervised sessions, and remain to be confirmed in regular operation.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background of the Study}
\label{cr:background}

A queue management system organizes the order in which people receive a
service. A basic system gives each person a number and shows which
number is being served; more advanced systems can also show queue
position, estimate waiting time, and help staff see when demand is
building. At a college cashiering office, this information lets
students plan where to wait and helps staff manage the line during busy
periods. The system is most useful when its displayed status reflects
the people who have actually arrived and the service being provided at
the counters.

Queue management inefficiency is a recognized challenge in service delivery organizations worldwide. Research from banking, healthcare, government service desks, and academic institutions consistently documents that manual queue processes cannot provide real-time status visibility, accurate wait-time estimates, or adaptive counter management when demand increases rapidly~\cite{nair2025survey,batcharo2025smart,yifter2023modeling}. As organizations in various sectors have introduced automated queue systems—which combine digital ticketing, camera-based monitoring, and predictive analytics—the operational gap between manual and automated queue management has widened~\cite{rashid2023smartqueue,zou2026customer}. In these settings, the absence of automated observation and prediction leaves both service staff and clients without the information needed to manage or navigate the service flow efficiently.

In the Philippines, this gap is visible across higher education institutions (HEIs). Enrollment, payment, and clearance cycles bring concentrated student arrivals to service windows within compressed periods, creating queue conditions that manual staff management cannot easily absorb. Philippine studies document the persistence of long enrollment queues and the operational pressure that rising student volumes place on service windows~\cite{concepcion2026smart,mallari2022clique,rrlcomp023automatedenrollmentqueueing,rrlcomp025lineuclmenhancing}. Existing Philippine HEI queue solutions primarily address web-based ticketing and appointment-booking rather than real-time passive observation of student arrivals at service areas.

At Naga College Foundation, Inc.\ (NCF), informal accounts from students and service staff indicate that wait times during enrollment, payment, and clearance periods can become substantial, with congestion most pronounced during the first weeks of each semester. Enrollment, cashiering, and library services are separate offices, but students often experience them as one connected process, so a delay at one window affects the next transaction the student must complete. This pattern, combined with the absence of a real-time queue display and manual recording of transaction order, reflects the same kind of service delivery inefficiency that the literature identifies as a recurring issue in Philippine HEIs. The Commission on Higher Education recognizes accessibility and efficient service delivery as indicators of institutional quality~\cite{ched2022}; for this reason, improving queue visibility is treated in this study as part of improving the student service experience, not merely as a counter-management task.

Conventional queue systems begin only after a student has requested a
number or staff have encoded the transaction: they store tickets, but
they do not observe the service area, confirm arrivals, or anticipate
changes in the line. The gap is the missing link among arrival
detection, staff monitoring, student status access, and prediction.

\sysname{} responds with a camera-connected queue management prototype
whose central mechanism is identification rather than counting. After
agreeing to a data-privacy notice, a student voluntarily enrols a face
signature in the mobile application, requests a number with a payment
slip when needed, and is issued the number automatically when the
queue-zone camera recognizes them, with no manual entry at the window.
Detection uses YOLOv8n, which detects objects in a single network
pass~\cite{redmon2018} and is light enough for prototype hardware while
supporting persistent tracking~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack,jocher2023,wang2025lightweight};
newer variants such as YOLOv11 and
YOLOv12~\cite{jocher2024yolo11,tian2025yolov12} were not adopted because
the study prioritizes stable person detection in one queue zone over
marginal accuracy gains that need more computing resources.
Identification compares each face's embedding with those of the enrolled
students and accepts a match only when it clears an absolute similarity
threshold \emph{and} leads the next-best candidate by a margin; otherwise
the person is referred to staff, and people who have not enrolled are
never identified.

A FastAPI backend and MySQL database connect the detector, the queue
records, the staff dashboard, the mobile application, and a prediction
module that estimates each student's wait. For NCF, the prototype
answers a concrete operational question: whether a camera-fed queue
record can give staff and students a clearer view of the line than
manual counter updates.

The Review of Related Literature therefore examines computer vision for
observing the queue, face recognition with open-set refusal for
identifying enrolled students, queue management for ticketing and
identity maintenance, and forecasting for the wait-time estimates that
staff and students rely on.

\section{Review of Related Studies}
\label{cr:rrl}

\subsection{Computer Vision for Real-Time Person Detection}
Recent computer-vision work shows why camera-based queue observation is
technically feasible. Sindagi and Patel~\cite{sindagi2018survey}
surveyed CNN-based crowd counting and density estimation, noting the
move from scale-limited approaches to models that handle occlusion,
complex scenes, and dense groups more reliably. One-stage detectors
such as YOLO process a frame quickly enough for live
monitoring~\cite{redmon2018}, and Terven et~al.~\cite{terven2023yolo}
trace the family from YOLOv1 to YOLOv8 and YOLO-NAS with attention to
the accuracy-speed trade-off in each generation. Varghese and
Sambath~\cite{rrlcomp092yolov8novelobject} document YOLOv8's gains in
performance and robustness over earlier generations, and lightweight
variants extend it to constrained hardware: a YOLOv8n-derived person
detector for edge surveillance~\cite{wang2025lightweight}, an
attention-based design with better small-target recall at fewer
parameters~\cite{rrlcomp091improvedyolov8lightweight}, and SGST-YOLOv8
for real-time detection of small, partially occluded persons on
campus~\cite{rrlcomp016sgstyolov8improved}. A comparison of YOLOv8 and
YOLOv11 for person detection in crowded
scenes~\cite{rrlcomp019evaluatingyolov8yolov11} found YOLOv8's mature
tooling and tracking integration the more practical for deployment.
Together these support a lightweight YOLOv8n base for the \sysname{}
prototype.

People counting built on such detectors has been validated in practice.
A YOLOv8 pipeline counts people in real time without specialized
hardware~\cite{rrlcomp001realtimepeople}; detection combined with
tracking yields density estimates over time in public
spaces~\cite{rrlcomp031peoplecountingpublic} and in urban environments
with overlapping boxes and perspective
distortion~\cite{rrlcomp032visionpeoplecounting}; and video analysis of
visitors holds up under the lighting variation and occlusion of live
feeds~\cite{rrlcomp033deeplearningvideo}. Crowd anomaly research adds
two cautions for queue-zone filtering: temporal context remains an open
challenge~\cite{rrlcomp034deepcrowdanomaly}, and model complexity trades
against latency in live systems~\cite{rrlcomp042surveydeeplearning}. A
YOLOv8 framework for dense-crowd behaviour
detection~\cite{rrlcomp043enhancedframeworkreal} shows that individuals
can still be separated at high density when the detection zone and
confidence thresholds are tuned, and pedestrian attribute
recognition~\cite{rrlcomp020frameworkpedestrianattribute} shows how box
features can be extended to classification beyond localization.

Because \sysname{} uses a camera, privacy is a design constraint.
Padilla-Lopez et~al.~\cite{padillalopez2015privacy} survey visual
privacy protection such as camera-level anonymization, selective
scrambling, and aggregate-only transmission. Later work anonymizes video
with low latency without preventing crowd
detection~\cite{rrlcomp013lowlatencyvideo}, protects footage at the edge
so that identifiable images need not be
transmitted~\cite{rrlcomp021privacypreservingsurveillance}, and
suppresses personal identifiers before storage for GDPR
compliance~\cite{rrlcomp040deeplearninggdpr}. User studies find that
acceptance of smart surveillance depends on transparency about data use
and on the absence of facial
recognition~\cite{rrlcomp022perceptionrisksusefulness}, a finding that
bears directly on a system that does use it. \sysname{} works with
derived data wherever it can: bounding boxes for detection and face
embeddings for identification. No video archive or enrolment photograph
is kept, and camera frames reach the staff dashboard only as the latest
annotated snapshot, which is overwritten rather than stored
(Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

Detection in a queue is not a per-frame problem: a number must stay with
the same person across frames, so detection is paired with tracking.
YOLOv8 has been combined with DeepSORT for the person
class~\cite{rrlcomp003persondetectionproposed}, with
OC-SORT~\cite{rrlcomp017multiobjectpedestrian} and an improved
association stage~\cite{rrlcomp018multiobjectivepedestrian} for more
consistent identities under crowding and occlusion, with a sliding
innovation filter~\cite{rrlcomp098objectdetectiontracking}, and with
centroid tracking for directional
monitoring~\cite{rrlcomp099realtimewrong}, the last showing how a zone
rule can be layered on ordinary tracking output, as \sysname{} does with
its queue zone. Association rests on motion prediction, where Kalman
filtering remains standard and has been refined with
adaptive~\cite{rrlcomp061adaptivekalmanfilter},
unscented~\cite{rrlcomp062achievingadaptivevisual}, and
reliability-weighted~\cite{rrlcomp063robustvisualtracking}
formulations. Studies of bounding-box dynamics and noise
covariance~\cite{rrlcomp064boundingboxdynamics} show that the box itself
is a noisy measurement, which justifies smoothing it before it is used
for a zone decision.

Overlap is the remaining problem, since several boxes for one person
would inflate a queue count. Non-maximum suppression is the standard
remedy, and its limits in crowds are well documented: it may belong
later in a tracking pipeline than is
conventional~\cite{rrlcomp046nonmaximumsuppression}; multi-attribute NMS
targets crowds where real neighbours overlap
heavily~\cite{rrlcomp047multiattributenms}; soft suppression decays
rather than removes competing boxes~\cite{rrlcomp086enhancednmsdetection};
and spatial correlation~\cite{rrlcomp089spatialnonmaximum} and adaptive
thresholds for small objects~\cite{rrlcomp090adaptiveiouthresholding}
fit the overlap criterion to the scene. These findings inform the
overlap and duplicate-suppression gates in Chapter~3, which rest on the
same principle: a fixed global threshold fits poorly where people
legitimately stand close together.

\subsection{Face Recognition and Open-Set Identification}

Detection and tracking establish that a person is present but not who
the person is. Because \sysname{} issues a queue number to a named
enrolled student, it must identify rather than count, and the
face-recognition literature supplies both the representation and the
rule for trusting an identification.

\textbf{Embeddings and margins.} Schroff et~al.~\cite{schroff2015facenet}
established the approach \sysname{} follows: a network maps a face into
a vector space in which distance corresponds to identity, so identity is
stored as a short vector rather than a photograph. Surveys confirm that
this formulation unified the field~\cite{wang2021deepfacesurvey,facesurvey2022}.
Angular margins between identities made the space reliable: the
hypersphere margin of Liu et~al.~\cite{liu2017sphereface}, the
large-margin cosine loss of Wang et~al.~\cite{wang2018cosface}, and the
additive angular margin of ArcFace~\cite{deng2019arcface}, the model
\sysname{} uses, later refined with quality-aware
magnitudes~\cite{meng2021magface} and binary-classification
training~\cite{wen2021sphereface2}. \sysname{} applies the same margin
principle a second time, at the decision stage.

\textbf{Detection and modest hardware.} Recognition needs an aligned
face crop. Single-shot face detection with five landmarks normalises the
pose before the embedding is computed~\cite{deng2020retinaface}, and
lightweight detectors make CPU-only operation
viable~\cite{embeddedfacedetect2025}. MobileFaceNet, with under one
million parameters, approaches the accuracy of far larger
models~\cite{chen2018mobilefacenets} and is the recognition model
\sysname{} runs; architecture search with
distillation~\cite{pocketnet2022} and edge-specific
designs~\cite{edgeface2024} push further in the same direction.

\textbf{Deciding when not to answer.} Most people who pass the camera
are not enrolled, and a recognizer that always returns its closest match
will name them anyway. Scheirer et~al.~\cite{scheirer2013openset}
formalised open-set recognition and argued that a usable system must be
able to reject; Günther et~al.~\cite{gunther2017opensetface} applied this
to faces, and later work frames the question as whether the gallery
contains the probe at all~\cite{indexofmax2020}. These works justify the
acceptance rule of \sysname{}: an absolute threshold, a margin over the
runner-up, and refusal when either fails. Because refusal is a valid
outcome, a deployed recognizer is best described by its error rate at
the operating point in force~\cite{operatingpoint2026}; accuracy varies
across demographic groups~\cite{grother2019frvt}, image quality predicts
the outcome~\cite{terhorst2020serfiq,boutros2023crfiqa}, which is why a
detector-confidence gate precedes matching, and occlusion degrades
it~\cite{maskedreview2025}.

\textbf{Threats and precedents.} A presentation attack substitutes a
photograph for a live face~\cite{yu2024antispoofing}, and \sysname{} does
not implement liveness detection, a stated limitation; a leaked template
cannot be reissued, which template-protection schemes
address~\cite{rathgeb2021templateprotection}, while \sysname{} relies on
the non-invertibility of the embedding and on access control. Face
recognition is in routine use for classroom
attendance~\cite{patel2025classroomcctv,transferattendance2021} and
campus access~\cite{campusaccess2024}. The closest precedent, Handaga
et~al.~\cite{handaga2019queue}, used it to remove the line formed when
students recorded attendance one at a time; \sysname{} instead issues a
queue number, which raises the cost of a wrong identification and is why
the refusal rule is its central design question.

\textbf{Proportionality.} Deploying biometric identification must be
proportionate to its purpose~\cite{negri2024deploy}; the European
Union's AI Act treats it as high-risk, requiring transparency and human
oversight~\cite{aiactbiometric2025}; and public acceptance depends on
consent and auditability~\cite{remotebiometric2024}. In \sysname{},
enrolment is voluntary and preceded by consent, recognition is confined
to the queue zone and to students who asked for service, unenrolled
persons are refused, and no photograph is retained
(Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\subsection{Queue Management and Person Re-Identification}
Beyond detection, a queue system must recognise a person who leaves the
camera's view and returns, and restore their place rather than issue a
new number. Ye et~al.~\cite{ye2022reid} survey deep person
re-identification in closed- and open-world settings along three axes:
feature representation, metric learning, and ranking. Later
reviews~\cite{wang2024reid,asperti2025reid} document a shift toward
Transformer-based, self-supervised, and unsupervised methods with better
robustness to occlusion and pose. Surveys of occluded re-identification
categorise methods by occlusion
type~\cite{rrlcomp006deeplearningoccluded} and note the gap between
benchmark results and the irregular occlusion of real
deployments~\cite{rrlcomp007occludedpersonidentification}. Other work
aligns heterogeneous appearance representations across
modalities~\cite{rrlcomp079crossmodalityperson} and improves matching at
low resolution~\cite{rrlcomp088personidentificationenhanced}, a concern
where the camera sits far from the counter.

Practical re-identification systems combine appearance, from colour
histograms to deep embeddings, with spatio-temporal cues such as last
position and elapsed time. Features from overlapping body stripes give
robust identification without deep metric
learning~\cite{rrlcomp078personidentificationdiscriminative}; colour
histograms fused with trajectory models reduce error behind
obstacles~\cite{rrlcomp058fusingappearancespatio}; multi-camera tracking
shows that without a shared view, matching must lean on strongly
discriminative features~\cite{rrlcomp073unveilingpersonnelmovement}; and
a wildlife-monitoring study pairs a Kalman filter with HSV colour and
Pearson correlation~\cite{rrlcomp077smartwildlifemonitoring}. The
pre-oral design of \sysname{} drew on this family, using a split-body
colour signature supported by recency windows and spatial fallbacks. It
was replaced after the pre-oral defence for a structural reason: a
clothing signature identifies an appearance, not a person. Two students
in the same uniform cannot be told apart by it, the same student changes
clothes between visits, and nothing in it confirms that the person at
the camera is the account holder. Once the system had to name a
particular enrolled student, no amount of fusion could make appearance
sufficient, so \sysname{} took the deep-embedding branch of this
tradition and applied it to the face. One comparison rule then serves
both identification and re-entry, and the compensating mechanisms are no
longer needed.

The multi-object tracking literature addresses the same problem within a
single scene. A survey~\cite{rrlcomp005deeplearningmulti} organises the
field by how detections are associated across frames, the decision a
queue tracker makes when a new box may continue an existing entry. For
crowds, finite-tailed updating~\cite{rrlcomp048effectivelyfinitetailed}
handles many competing candidates, predictive fusion with adaptive
measurement~\cite{rrlcomp049multiobjecttracking} combines motion and
appearance evidence, global object
models~\cite{rrlcomp072effectivemultiobject} reason over the whole
scene, and occlusion-aware
tracking~\cite{rrlcomp057dettrackalgorithmmultiple} reduces identity
fragmentation when people briefly block one another. Three-dimensional
distance-based overlap~\cite{rrlcomp076dioudistanceintersection} and
ultrafast architectures~\cite{rrlcomp097activemultiobject} show where the
field is heading, beyond what a single-zone prototype needs. This work
also sets a boundary: tracking holds an identity only while a session
lasts, and loses it when a track drops for long enough or the camera
restarts. \sysname{} therefore uses the tracker to keep the line in
order and the face to establish who each person is across visits.

At the system level, camera-based queue tracking has been used to
estimate waiting time from detected queue
length~\cite{rrlcomp008realtimequeue}, and digital ticketing in public
transport reduces manual errors and speeds up
processing~\cite{rrlcomp056efficientpublictransport}, paralleling the
ticket generation in \sysname{}. Queueing studies model arrivals that
change with service ratings~\cite{rrlcomp067selfservicerating}, relevant
where student experience affects repeat visits, and Jencova
et~al.~\cite{rrlcomp068dimensioningoptimalnumber} apply M/M/c modelling
to choose the number of parallel service desks in airport passenger
handling, the model \sysname{} implements for counter staffing
decisions.

Philippine higher education institutions have adopted digital systems
for the same bottlenecks. Concepcion et~al.~\cite{concepcion2026smart}
describe a smart queuing and appointment system at Bulacan State
University that reduced wait times and improved perceived service
quality, but through user-initiated web ticketing and booking rather
than camera detection of arrivals, which is the gap \sysname{} targets.
Espinosa et~al.~\cite{rrlcomp023automatedenrollmentqueueing} and the
paperless Q-Line UCLM system~\cite{rrlcomp025lineuclmenhancing} show
that even simple digital queueing reduces waits relative to manual
systems; an evaluation of an enrollment information system in Southern
Philippines~\cite{rrlcomp041evaluationimprovementhei} highlights
usability and integration gaps; and automated admission
workflows~\cite{rrlcomp083automationclientservicing} reduce processing
time. Reviews of digital transformation in higher education identify
technology adoption, infrastructure readiness, and stakeholder buy-in as
the determinants of success~\cite{rrlcomp082digitaltransformationhigher},
and classify the organizational, technical, and cultural barriers that
explain why manual processes
persist~\cite{rrlcomp080classificationbarriersdigital}. Studies of
digital learning environments~\cite{rrlcomp081digitallearningdigital}
and of technology interfaces~\cite{rrlcomp085technologyinterfacestudent}
link students' experience with institutional technology to satisfaction
and retention, and emergent technologies improve administrative
throughput without proportional staffing
increases~\cite{rrlcomp084supportingeducationaladministration}.

\subsection{Predictive Analytics for Service Demand Forecasting}
Queue staff need two kinds of forecast: a current estimate for the
person already in line and a short look-ahead for counter planning.
Shmueli and Koppius~\cite{shmueli2011predictive} distinguish predictive
analytics from explanatory modelling by its test: whether empirical
models produce useful predictions for decisions.

The M/M/c queuing model provides analytical wait-time calculations from
real-time arrival and service rates, suited to current estimates and
near-term predictions~\cite{shortle2018fundamentals}. With Little's
Law~\cite{little2008law}, which relates the mean number in a system to
the arrival rate and the mean time spent in it, it gives \sysname{} the
basis for translating live arrival rates into expected waits. Analyses
of multi-server systems with heterogeneous service
rates~\cite{rrlcomp052analysismultiserver} and of Little's Law in
multiphase settings~\cite{rrlcomp051littleformulamultiphase} support
M/M/c as a practical baseline for cashiering counters, and empirical
work on queue configuration~\cite{song2024queue} shows that the
arrangement of a line, not only its length, shapes each person's wait,
which is why position and counter assignment are reported alongside a
single estimate. System utilization is
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \frac{\lambda}{c\mu}
  \label{eq:utilization}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the arrival rate, $\mu$ the service rate, and $c$ the
number of active servers. The mean number in the system with Little's
Law yields the expected wait used by the dashboard:
\begin{equation}
  W = \frac{L}{\lambda}
  \label{eq:littles_law}
\end{equation}
For medium-term forecasting, Holt's double exponential smoothing tracks
queue level and trend with separate smoothing
parameters~\cite{holt2004forecasting}, and Hyndman and
Athanasopoulos~\cite{hyndman2021forecasting} describe damping factors
that limit over-projection during temporary spikes, as when a line grows
during enrollment and thins once a batch is served. For the 30-minute
horizon, \sysname{} uses mean reversion to limit long-forecast
uncertainty.

Machine learning has been validated for waiting-time prediction in
several service settings. Even simple regression outperforms static
analytical estimates when historical data are
available~\cite{rrlcomp015machinelearningapproach}, and learned models
improve on M/M/c baselines when demand is
non-stationary~\cite{rrlcomp009waitingtimeprediction}. In hospitals,
adding patient dissatisfaction to queue length improves
prediction~\cite{rrlcomp050dissatisfactionconsideredwaiting},
treatment-time forecasts guide routing across service
counters~\cite{rrlcomp059parallelpatienttreatment}, and fairness across
patient groups must be assessed alongside
accuracy~\cite{rrlcomp014evaluatingfairnessmachine}; at airport
check-in, historical and real-time arrival data together give accurate
forecasts at multi-server
counters~\cite{rrlcomp069machinelearningprediction}. Mixture density
networks predict a distribution of delay rather than a single
value~\cite{rrlcomp012realtimeprediction}, and deep models handle
non-linear queue dynamics where classical models
underfit~\cite{rrlcomp036deeplearningassisted}. These works support
combining analytical and data-driven forecasts at different time
scales.

Among non-neural methods, exponential smoothing benefits substantially
from correct model selection~\cite{rrlcomp010exponentialsmoothingmodel}
and stays competitive with deep learning on short horizons with limited
data~\cite{rrlcomp011reviewmethodsmodels}, and gradient boosting captures
non-linear demand with few hyperparameters in
energy~\cite{rrlcomp035energyconsumptionforecasts} and
retail~\cite{rrlcomp044disaggregatedretailforecasting} forecasting.
Combining short-term smoothing with trend components aids operations
planning~\cite{rrlcomp070predictivesalesoperations}, and a hybrid of
LSTM and XGBoost improves on single methods at multi-step
horizons~\cite{rrlcomp071predictiveanalyticssales}. On evaluation,
Chicco et~al.~\cite{rrlcomp093coefficientdeterminationsquared} argue
that R\textsuperscript{2} is more informative than SMAPE, MAE, MAPE,
MSE, or RMSE taken in isolation and recommend reporting several metrics;
the choice of metric changes which model is
selected~\cite{rrlcomp094comparativeevaluationmodel}; and reviews in
wind-power~\cite{rrlcomp095evaluationmetricswind} and
energy~\cite{rrlcomp096performanceevaluationmachine} forecasting find
MAE and MAPE together more complete than either alone. MAPE has also
been used to evaluate ARIMA demand
forecasts~\cite{rrlcomp087predictiveanalyticsdemand}, and MAE to
evaluate predictions of student academic
success~\cite{rrlcomp100comparativeanalysismachine}, the latter in an
educational context.

Service-sector studies confirm both the approach and its limits. A
retrospective study that trained several machine-learning models on more
than three hundred thousand paediatric hospital
records~\cite{pediatricwait2025} found that the number of people
already waiting and the arrival rate were the strongest predictors of
waiting time, the same two quantities the \sysname{} prediction module
recovers from camera observation. Work on forecasting
emergency-department boarding time~\cite{boardingforecast2026} adds that
a prediction is useful only if it reaches staff early enough to change a
decision, which is why \sysname{} reports several horizons rather than a
single current estimate.

\section{Synthesis of the State-of-the-Art}
\label{cr:synthesis}

The reviewed studies point to a practical limitation of ordinary queue
records: an issued ticket list shows the order of numbers, but not who is
actually standing in the service area. Camera-based person detection
observes the queue as it forms, and YOLO detectors are fast enough to
watch a queue zone continuously. Within that family, the progression to
the attention-centric YOLOv12~\cite{tian2025yolov12} shows a widening gap
between research benchmarks and the constraints of a campus prototype,
which is why \sysname{} uses the lighter YOLOv8n with persistent
tracking. Detection alone is still not enough: tracking, duplicate
suppression, and presence confirmation decide whether a box is someone
genuinely waiting.

Detection and tracking also cannot say \emph{who} anyone is, and
\sysname{} must issue a number to a named enrolled student. The
embedding formulation~\cite{schroff2015facenet} and margin-based
training~\cite{wang2018cosface,deng2019arcface} make identity a
trustworthy comparison of short vectors, and the open-set
literature~\cite{scheirer2013openset,gunther2017opensetface} adds the
condition a service office operates under: most people passing the
camera are not enrolled. Identification in an open environment is
therefore a matter of a principled rule for declining to answer, and
\sysname{} treats refusal as a defined outcome routed to staff.

Queue management and forecasting research reinforce the need to move
beyond static ticket numbering: queuing theory and short-term
forecasting turn arrivals, open windows, and queue length into wait
estimates that students and staff can act on. Existing studies treat
these functions separately. Philippine HEI work such as Concepcion
et~al.~\cite{concepcion2026smart} addresses web-based ticketing rather
than camera-based operation, and face-recognition deployments in schools
such as the CCTV attendance system of
Patel et~al.~\cite{patel2025classroomcctv} identify students without
managing a queue. \sysname{} connects the four functions in one
prototype --- confirming who is present, identifying enrolled students
and issuing their numbers without manual input, showing each student
their position and wait, and giving staff wait estimates --- and is
evaluated on recognition accuracy, prediction accuracy, and user
satisfaction.

\section{Gap Bridged by the Study}
\label{cr:gap}

The literature reviewed shows strong individual contributions in queue
monitoring, object detection, face recognition, and wait-time
forecasting, but no existing study integrates these components into a
single campus-service prototype for a Philippine higher education
institution. Philippine HEI solutions, such as the web-based ticketing
system of Concepcion et~al.~\cite{concepcion2026smart}, do not include
passive camera-based arrival detection. Educational deployments of face
recognition, such as the CCTV attendance system of Patel
et~al.~\cite{patel2025classroomcctv}, record who was present rather than
issue a service entitlement. And while the open-set requirement is well
established in the recognition
literature~\cite{scheirer2013openset,gunther2017opensetface}, it is
ordinarily treated as a benchmark property rather than as an operating
rule with a defined consequence when the system declines to answer.

\sysname{} bridges this gap through a system that: (1)~establishes
empirical baseline data on the current problems of manual queue
management at NCF during busy periods, covering recorded wait times and
reported user experience; (2)~identifies enrolled students at the queue
zone from a face embedding and issues their queue number automatically,
with no manual input from staff or students, where detection locates the
person and recognition establishes which student they are; (3)~makes the
open-set requirement operational by accepting an identification only when
it clears an absolute similarity threshold \emph{and} leads the
next-best candidate by a stated margin, so that an ambiguous face is
referred to staff rather than resolved by the higher of two close scores;
(4)~employs a multi-horizon wait-time prediction module whose accuracy is
evaluated using MAE and MAPE; and (5)~assesses user satisfaction across
system usability, reliability, and clarity dimensions---directly
responding to each of the study's specific objectives.

staff see the line only by looking at it. \sysname{} rests on one main

theories, each of which explains one capability the system adds to that
line --- observing it, knowing who is in it, and anticipating how it will
change. Figure~\ref{fig:theoretical-framework} shows how the four

\emph{Queuing Theory} is the main theory of the study. In its standard
formulation~\cite{shortle2018fundamentals}, as recent studies of service
queues continue to apply it~\cite{yifter2023modeling}, a waiting line is
described by the arrival rate, the service rate, and the number of
counters open; the M/M/c model and Little's Law relate these to queue
length, utilisation, and waiting time. These are the quantities the
baseline survey found students unable to see. Queuing theory therefore
defines what \sysname{} must make visible, and its multi-server line is
what the wait-time estimate simulates.

\emph{Computer Vision Theory} explains how the line is observed without
a person watching it. Single-stage YOLO detectors locate every person in
a frame in one pass of a neural network~\cite{jocher2023}, and
multi-object tracking keeps one identity for each person across
frames~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack}. In \sysname{} this layer establishes
only that a person is present in the queue zone, not who the person is.

\emph{Deep Face Recognition Theory} explains how an enrolled student is
recognised. Deep metric learning maps each face to an embedding in which
photographs of the same person lie close together; ArcFace adds an
angular margin between identities so that two embeddings can be compared
by their cosine~\cite{deng2019arcface}, and recent surveys identify such
margin-based losses as the basis of current face
recognition~\cite{wang2021deepfacesurvey}. The theory explains why a
similarity score is meaningful but not when it is high enough to act on,
especially when the person at the camera may not be enrolled; the
threshold-and-margin rule this study contributes answers that question
(Statement of the Problem No.~2).

\emph{Forecasting Theory} explains how the line is anticipated rather
than only reported. Future values are estimated from structure in past
observations, and a forecast is judged by comparing it with what
actually happened~\cite{hyndman2021forecasting}, using the Mean Absolute
Error and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error applied under Statement of
the Problem No.~3.

Together, queuing theory states what the line needs to show, computer
vision establishes who is present, face recognition establishes which of
them are enrolled students entitled to a number, and forecasting
estimates how long each will wait. Their outputs reach users through the
staff dashboard, the mobile application, and the display board, which
are assessed under Statement of the Problem No.~4.

\textbf{Input.} The inputs are the live camera feed of the queue zone,
the face embeddings of students who agreed to the privacy notice and
enrolled, each student's request for a number with the payment slip,
the window configuration and service times, and the staff's actions.
Walk-ins are not an input: they keep taking a ticket from the office's
existing kiosk, which runs separately from \sysname{}.

\textbf{Process.} The camera pipeline detects, tracks, and confirms the
people standing in the queue zone. Each confirmed face is compared with
the enrolled roster under the threshold-and-margin rule, and anyone not
identified with confidence is left for staff rather than guessed at. A
recognised student who asked for a number receives one, with a wait
estimate and a ticket, and is later called to a window by the cashier
or, in automatic mode, by the system. Unused accounts are archived and
deleted under the retention policy.

\textbf{Output and Outcome.} Recognised students receive a number
without manual input and follow their place, wait, and turn on their
phone; staff see the queue, the windows, the payment slips, and alerts;
and the display board announces each call. The intended outcome answers
the problems found under Statement of the Problem No.~1 and is measured
by Statements of the Problem No.~2 to No.~4.

\textbf{Feedback.} Measured service times and waits, window changes, and
staff decisions return to the input stage, so that the wait estimate and
the queue reflect the office as it is operating.

\section{Statement of the Problem}
\label{cr:sop}

The study is guided by the following research problems:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What are the current problems with the manual queue management
        process during busy periods, regarding wait times and
        user-experience?
  \item What is the level of accuracy of the face-recognition system
        in identifying enrolled students and assigning queue numbers
        without requiring manual input?
  \item What is the level of accuracy of the system's wait-time
        prediction model in terms of Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and
        Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)?
  \item What is the level of user's satisfaction in terms of:
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item[(a)] system's usability;
          \item[(b)] system's reliability; and
          \item[(c)] system clarity?
        \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Assumptions}
\label{cr:assumptions}

The following assumptions delimit the proposed evaluation:

\textbf{Camera visibility and queue-zone coverage.} The camera covers the
queue zone with enough resolution and lighting for YOLO to detect the
people standing in it and for their faces to be detected and recognised
at queue-zone distance, under the lighting and crowd levels typical of
NCF service areas.

\textbf{Stable tracking.} Person tracking keeps each person's identity
stable for as long as they stand in the queue zone, so that their face
can be captured and matched once, and a student who briefly leaves and
returns can be recognised again by face.

\textbf{Student participation.} Students who want a system-issued queue
number have an Android phone and an NCF Gbox account, enrol their face
voluntarily through the mobile application, and ask to be queued before
they reach the queue zone.

\textbf{Representative enrolment.} A student's enrolment photographs
reflect how they look when they queue; a large change in appearance, such
as a face covering not worn at enrolment, may lead the system to refer
them to staff rather than recognise them.

\textbf{Network availability.} The service office has a working internet
connection to the cloud-hosted backend during the evaluation period,
since recognition and queue-number issuance depend on it.

\textbf{Usefulness of prediction outputs.} The implemented prediction
models generate forecasts that are actionable and sufficiently accurate
for staff scheduling and counter management decisions, with accuracy
assessed using MAE and MAPE in Chapter 3.

\textbf{Ticket rendering.} The ticket record remains the same whether
it is printed on thermal paper or rendered as a PDF: both outputs carry
the queue number, short code, QR link, and JWT used for status
checking.

\textbf{Staff capability and system adoption.} Service staff and
administrators possess basic digital literacy to operate the web
dashboard and interpret the system's outputs.

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:scope}

This section describes the boundaries of the \sysname{} study, organized
according to its introduction, scope, and limitations.

\textbf{Introduction.} \sysname{} is a camera-assisted queue management
prototype developed and evaluated for one NCF service-office setting in
Naga City, Philippines. It addresses the specific problem of manual,
observation-based queue handling in a campus service office by combining
automated person detection, face-based identification of enrolled
students, queue-number assignment, ticket generation, and wait-time
prediction in a single prototype system.

\textbf{Scope.} The study covers the development and evaluation of the
\sysname{} prototype in the selected NCF service office. The build
includes the YOLOv8n detection pipeline, self-service face enrolment and
ArcFace-based identification of enrolled students, face-based re-entry
matching, short- and medium-horizon wait-time prediction, FastAPI
endpoints, MySQL storage, a staff dashboard, a student mobile client, and
printer-agnostic ticket generation. It also covers the features added for
the final defense: a payment-slip form in the mobile client whose lines
and total are shown to the cashier; staff calling of the next number to
their own window, with an optional automatic mode; notices that tell the
student who is being served, when it is their turn and at which window,
and when their number was skipped; a data-privacy consent step before
registration; a two-stage data-retention policy; and face matching sized
for an enrolment of 10,000 students. During office use, the ticket record
is meant to be printed through a thermal printer. During evaluation, it
is rendered as a PDF so the queue number, short code, QR link, and JWT
validation can be checked without relying on printer availability.

\textbf{Limitations.} The present build uses one camera-defined queue
zone and one selected service office. It excludes multi-zone and
multi-department operation, and no priority queue is implemented in the
system; every queue entry is served in ordinary queue order without
priority handling based on student status or urgency. Face-based
identification and system-issued queue numbers apply only to students
who have enrolled voluntarily and asked to be queued; walk-ins and
unenrolled visitors are detected but never identified, and keep the
printed kiosk number they receive today. The system does not test
whether a face belongs to a live person, so it cannot distinguish a
student from a photograph of that student. The build also excludes
direct links to the Campus Management System or Student Information
System and IoT inputs other than the camera feed and ticket printer.

\sysname{} requires a working internet connection. Its backend,
database, cache, and ticket storage are cloud-hosted, and the detector
in the office must reach the backend for every recognition and every
queue number issued; if the connection fails, recognition and number
issuance stop until it returns. Offline operation is outside the scope
of this build. The user evaluation is limited to a supervised
trial session held before the final defense, not a semester of regular
operation, and the trial was conducted on a test installation on the
NCF campus rather
than at the cashiering office itself.

\label{cr:trc-scope}The face check at the camera also bounds what \sysname{} changes about
the line. A student who has asked for a number must still stand in the
queue zone and be recognised before the number is issued, so the system
does not remove the physical line at the camera: when many students ask
at once, they are recognised one after another as they enter the zone.
The school's Technology Resources Center (TRC), which reviewed the system
on 10~November~2026, raised this point and suggested issuing the number
directly from the mobile application and using the camera only to
observe the room. That redesign is outside this study. Identifying the
student by camera before a number is issued is the computer-vision
component required of this Computer Science capstone and approved by the
panel, and the adviser decided to keep it. From the same review, the
study adopted the payment-slip form, clearer wording in the mobile
application, and a loading screen; the app-only number with a passive
camera is listed among the recommendations
(Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}). Other services the TRC mentioned,
such as a document-request organizer for the Registrar, are also outside
the scope.

\label{cr:gb-scope}The wait-time estimate shown to students is produced by simulating the
line window by window, with a regression on the number of people ahead
as its benchmark and fallback. A Gradient Boosting predictor was also
trained on the historical cashiering log and evaluated offline (MAE 2.35
minutes on held-out dates), but it is not part of the deployed system.
On the available data, learned models did not predict waits better than
the simpler rule based on the number of people ahead; the planned
runtime calibration of the Gradient Boosting output, using the day's
service speed, the previous day's wait at the same hour, and recent
prediction error, was not implemented; and loading the model's
libraries would use about 100~MB of the backend's 512~MB of memory.
Whether a learned model can improve on the simulation is left to future
work, once the system's own log of measured waits is large enough to
train and test one (Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}).

\section{Significance of the Study}

The value of the study is in how the prototype supports everyday
service-office work: it gives staff a clearer view of the line,
provides students with a way to check their queue status, and keeps
records that administrators can review when improving service flow.

\textbf{Service Staff.} Service staff receive a dashboard that shows
the active queue, individual wait times, counter assignments, and
short-horizon demand forecasts without requiring them to recount the
line manually.

\textbf{Students.} Students benefit from a queueing process that removes
the counter-side step of asking for a number. A student enrols once, from
their own phone, by signing in with their institutional account and
capturing a face signature. On any later visit they request a place in
the queue in the mobile client and then walk into the monitored area,
where the camera recognizes them and the number is issued without staff
involvement or manual entry. Status is then followed in the mobile client
or through the ticket credential. Because the number is issued only to a
student who has asked for one, recognition alone never places anybody in
the queue.

\textbf{Institutional Administrators.} Administrators receive queue
performance records that can be used when reviewing staffing, service
hours, and physical layout.

\textbf{Naga College Foundation, Inc.} The institution benefits from a
campus-oriented prototype that demonstrates how automation, computer
vision, and predictive analytics can support service delivery and
student-facing operations.

\textbf{Academic Community and Future Researchers.} The work provides a
local reference for combining computer vision, re-identification,
ticket generation, and predictive analytics in Philippine HEI service
queues. Future researchers may extend it to multi-camera zones,
priority queues, direct campus-system integration, or alternative
detection and forecasting models.

\section{Definition of Terms}
\label{cr:definitions}

The following terms are defined operationally as applied in this study,
so that the system and its evaluation are read consistently throughout
the manuscript. Implementation details of each routine are specified in
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}.

\textbf{ArcFace Embedding.} The 512-number representation of a face
produced by the ArcFace model in the InsightFace \texttt{buffalo\_s}
pack. The cosine similarity between two embeddings expresses how likely
they belong to the same person, and a face cannot be reconstructed from
its embedding.

\textbf{Call Next.} The staff action that calls the first waiting
number to the window held by that staff account. In the optional
automatic mode, the system calls numbers to free windows itself.

\textbf{Data Retention Policy.} The rule that limits how long student
data stays in the system. An account with no sign-in or queue request for
one year is archived, which deactivates it and erases its face
signature, and an account archived for one more year is deleted. Staff
can apply either stage earlier, for example to graduating students.

\textbf{Face Enrolment.} The voluntary process by which a student
becomes known to the recognizer: the student signs in with an NCF Gbox
account, agrees to the data-privacy notice, and submits one to five
photographs, from which one averaged ArcFace embedding is stored. The
photographs are discarded and never written to the database.

\textbf{Join Intent.} The student's request for a queue number, made by
tapping \emph{Get a Queue Number} and filling in the payment slip in the
mobile client. A recognised student who has not made this request is
issued nothing.

\textbf{Mobile Client.} The Flutter application through which a student
signs in, enrols a face, requests a number, and follows their own
position, estimated wait, and turn.

\textbf{Open-Set Rejection.} The requirement that the recognizer refuse
any face it has not enrolled rather than name the closest match, so that
unenrolled visitors pass through the queue zone without being
identified.

\textbf{Payment Slip.} The form in the mobile client, laid out like the
cashier's paper Payment Slip, on which the student states what they are
paying and the amount of each line; the lines and the TOTAL are shown to
the cashier when the student is called.

\textbf{Presence Confirmation.} The check that a detected person is
standing in the queue rather than passing by: the centre of their box
must stay inside the queue zone for 14 updates with a motion test, or,
once their face is captured, for one second with three face updates.

\textbf{Public Display Board.} The browser page in the waiting area that
shows the active queue and announces each call by voice, using the
browser's built-in speech synthesis.

\textbf{Queue Zone.} The rectangular region of the camera frame, set by
four pixel coordinates, inside which a person must stand to be counted
in the queue.

\textbf{Threshold-and-Margin Decision Rule.} The two-part test every
face match must pass: the similarity to the best-matching enrolled
student must reach $\tau_{\mathrm{match}} = 0.30$ and exceed the
second-best by $\tau_{\mathrm{margin}} = 0.15$. A face that passes the
threshold but not the margin is refused rather than guessed.

\textbf{Wait-Time Estimate.} The minutes a student is expected to wait
before being called, computed by simulating the line window by window
from the order of the line, the windows open, and the measured service
time.

\textbf{You Only Look Once (YOLO).} The YOLOv8n person
detector~\cite{jocher2023}, used with ByteTrack
tracking~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack} to detect and follow each person in
the camera frames.

\chapter{Methodology}

Chapter~2 sets out how the \sysname{} prototype was built and evaluated
for the selected NCF service office. The chapter covers
the research design, respondents and locale, sampling procedure, data
gathering, research instruments, questionnaire validation, ethical
safeguards, statistical treatment, system architecture, development
process, and testing plan.

\section{Research Design}

The research design has two linked parts. The developmental part covers
the construction of \sysname{}; the evaluation part checks how the
prototype behaves in a supervised trial. Quantitative evidence
comes from the system's own logs, offline tests on photographs of known
identity, manual observation of cashier transactions, a load test, and
survey ratings. These data are used to measure face-recognition
accuracy, the issuance of queue numbers without manual input, backend
response under load, and wait-time prediction error through Mean
Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The
five-point Likert survey records how cashier staff and students rate
the system's usability, reliability, and clarity after using it. Qualitative evidence comes from the open-ended questions of both
questionnaires, where respondents can explain problems, concerns, or
useful features that a rating scale may not capture.

The engineering work is organized around the prototype components that
carry the main technical risk: YOLOv8n detection, face-based
identification and re-entry matching, ticket-payload generation,
multi-horizon prediction, the FastAPI backend, the staff dashboard, and
the student mobile application. Each component was built through the
Agile sprint cycle described in Section~\ref{sec:sdp}, and its output
was checked against the design principles in
Section~\ref{sec:design-arch}. Quantitative results and qualitative
accounts are analyzed side by side so that the discussion can compare
measured performance with the actual experience of the users.

\section{Population and Locale of the Study}
\label{cr:population}

Naga College Foundation, Inc.\ (NCF), located in Naga City, Camarines
Sur, Philippines, serves as the locale of this study. NCF was selected
as the research site because its service offices, particularly the
cashiering office, experience high student traffic during enrollment,
payment, and clearance periods, creating visible peak-demand conditions
that are appropriate for evaluating a camera-assisted queue management
prototype. The prototype was designed for the cashiering office, the
single-queue-zone site defined in the Scope and Delimitation of
Chapter~1. It was not deployed there during this study: the supervised
trial was conducted on a test installation of the camera, detector, and
queue zone on the NCF campus (Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}).

The study population consists of the two groups who directly use the
\sysname{} prototype. The first is the cashier staff assigned to the
selected cashiering office counter, who operate the staff dashboard. The
second is currently enrolled NCF students who transact at the cashiering
office and receive a \sysname{}-issued queue number. Administrators and
IT personnel are not surveyed: they oversee the office but do not use the
system themselves, so they could not rate its usability, reliability, or
clarity from experience. The total size of each group is being
confirmed with NCF administration; the number of respondents drawn from
each group is reported with the results in Chapter~3.

\section{Sample and Sampling Technique}
\label{cr:sampling}

This study uses total enumeration for cashier staff and convenience
sampling for students, adapted to the supervised trial session in which
the system is evaluated. Sampling is adapted to the size and role of
each group rather than applying one procedure to both, so that the
evaluation includes the operational perspective of staff and the user
experience of students.

\textbf{Service staff.} Because the population of staff directly
involved in queue-based transactions in the selected service office is
relatively small and bounded, total enumeration is used; all eligible
staff are invited to participate. The inclusion criterion is
having used the staff dashboard before answering the questionnaire.

\textbf{Students.} Student respondents are drawn by convenience
sampling from the students who volunteer for the supervised trial
session. Each goes through the full sequence: signing in, enrolling a
face, asking to be queued, and being issued a queue number at the queue
zone. The inclusion criterion is completing that sequence at least once
during the session. This replaces the Slovin-sized sample stratified by
year level in the original design, as explained in
Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}; the student sample is therefore smaller
and is not claimed to represent all NCF students.

\textbf{Baseline respondents.} The baseline survey for Statement of the
Problem No.~1 is a separate sample drawn by convenience sampling. Links
to the online questionnaire were shared with service staff and with
students of NCF, and those who chose to answer formed the sample. Seven
staff members answered from 16 to 23 September 2026. The student form,
revised to add questions on current practice, the current display, and
face registration, received 155 submissions on 3 October and from 11 to
16 November 2026; after excluding invalid and unconfirmed submissions
(Section~\ref{sec:sop1-results}), 83 students are analysed. Because
respondents selected themselves, the sample may over-represent people
with stronger views about the queue, and it was not sized by Slovin's
formula; it is particularly small on the staff side. Its results are therefore
read as evidence of what the current problems are and how strongly each
group feels them, not as estimates for the whole NCF population.

\textbf{User acceptance test respondents.} The UAT sample is also a
convenience sample: the forty-two students who took part in the UAT
session on 16~November~2026, by using the mobile application or watching
it used, and answered the UAT questionnaire
(Section~\ref{sec:uat-results}). No cashier staff took part.

\textbf{Exclusion criterion.} In both groups, any prospective
respondent below 18 years of age is excluded. The age criterion is
enforced through a screening question that immediately precedes the
informed consent form, and any prospective respondent indicating an age
below 18 is thanked for their interest and not enrolled.

\section{Data Gathering Procedure}
\label{sec:datagathering}

Data gathering is divided into three phases so that the manual
baseline, live system behavior, and user feedback are not mixed
together prematurely. The sequence also makes it possible to compare
what staff normally do, what \sysname{} records during use, and what
participants report after using the prototype.

\textbf{Phase 1: Setup and Operational Baseline.} Before the trial, the
camera placement, queue-zone calibration, counter configuration, and
initial seed value for the average service time are set. The seed value
is used by the prediction service until enough
transactions have been logged for the dynamic service-time measurement
procedure (Section~\ref{subsec:service-time}) to supply a measured
estimate. The existing manual process was observed directly: 166 cashier
transactions at Windows~3, 4, and~5 were recorded by manual observation
on 8, 9, and 22 September 2026, as described at the end of this
section. The cashier staff were consulted during this data collection
and were invited to take part in the evaluation. The baseline questionnaire described in
Section~\ref{sec:instrument} was administered online through shared
questionnaire links, to staff from 16 to 23 September 2026 and, in its
revised student form, to students on 3 October and from 11 to 16
November 2026; because \sysname{} was not deployed at the office, the
students' answers still describe the current process. Its results
answer Statement of the Problem No.~1. These baseline accounts establish
the reference against which the \sysname{}-mediated process is
compared.

\textbf{Phase 2: Supervised Trial and Performance Logging.} After alpha
testing (Section~2.10), the system was operated in a supervised trial.
It was not deployed at the cashiering office; the trial was conducted on
a test installation of the camera, detector, and queue zone on the NCF
campus, with
volunteer students asking to be queued from the mobile application and
standing in the camera's queue zone. Throughout its operation
the system
automatically logs every detection event, recognition decision, queue
assignment, ticket issuance, prediction output, and dashboard action,
with timestamps. These records are the source of the live results for
Statement of the Problem No.~2. The original design added a second
stream: an independent observer keeping a manual ground-truth log of
each actual queue entrant, each re-entry, and each actual wait. That log
was not kept, so the live results are the system's own record rather
than measurements against an observer. The measures that depend on it
are defined in Section~2.7 and are recommended for the evaluation
proposed in Chapter~4.

\textbf{Phase 3: Post-Trial Survey.} After the supervised
trial, the questionnaire in Section~2.5 was administered through an
online form to the respondent groups identified in Section~2.3;
students answered from 25 to 28 September 2026. The cashier staff
answered on 27 and 28 September 2026, after a short hands-on session
with the staff dashboard. The form collects
no names, and responses are analyzed in aggregate. The original design also invited a subset of respondents to
a short semi-structured interview. The interviews were not conducted,
so the open-ended questions in Section~IV of the questionnaire are the
only qualitative evidence from users of the system.

\textbf{Phase 4: User Acceptance Testing.}\label{cr:uat} After the features added for
the final defense were completed, a user acceptance test (UAT) was
conducted with students on 16~November~2026 in a laboratory on the NCF
campus, facilitated by a BS Information Systems student. The researchers
presented the system with a diorama of the cashiering office and
explained how it works; the students then tried the mobile application,
signing in with Gbox, giving consent, registering their faces, and
joining the queue with the payment-slip form, while others watched these
steps being performed. Because the session time was limited, the
queue-zone detector was not run and no queue number was issued; the
steps that depend on it were shown only on the diorama. Every student
then answered the UAT questionnaire (Appendix~\ref{app:uat}), a revision
of the user-evaluation questionnaire whose statements name the steps of
the system. The system's own log for the session --- accounts created,
face signatures saved, and face sign-in attempts --- was examined
alongside the answers. Cashier staff did not take part in the UAT. The
results are evaluated by a statistician, whose report accompanies the
results chapter.

\textbf{Shortened evaluation protocol.} The original design allowed at
least two weeks of regular operation before the user evaluation, a
separate pilot of 10 to 15 respondents before the main survey, a
student sample sized by Slovin's formula, content validation of the
questionnaire by three expert raters, an independent observer's
ground-truth log, and follow-up interviews. The time available before
the final defense did not allow these, so the protocol was shortened in
six ways, each reported here rather than left implicit. First, the two
weeks of regular operation at the cashiering office are replaced by
supervised trial use of the system on a test installation on the NCF
campus, and the
questionnaire is answered immediately afterwards.
Second, the separate pilot is replaced by Cronbach's alpha computed on
the evaluation responses themselves. Third, the student sample consists
of the volunteers who complete the trial rather than a Slovin-sized
sample. Fourth, the content validation by expert raters was not carried
out: no rater reviewed the items and no Content Validity Index was
computed. Fifth, the independent observer's ground-truth log was not
kept. Sixth, the follow-up interviews were not conducted. The
consequences for interpretation are direct. The results for Statement
of the Problem No.~4 describe users' first experience of \sysname{}
under supervision, not their satisfaction after sustained daily use,
and they rest on items that were checked for internal consistency but
not reviewed by experts. The live results for Statement of the Problem
No.~2 are the system's own record, which is why recognition accuracy is
measured offline on photographs of known identity
(Section~\ref{subsec:heldout-accuracy}). All results are reported with
these qualifications.

\textbf{Change to the Statement of the Problem No.~3 evaluation.} The
original design measured prediction accuracy during live operation, by
comparing the system's estimates at the current, 5-minute, 15-minute,
and 30-minute horizons with stopwatch waits. That comparison requires
the system to be running at the counter while an observer times each
student, and the time available before submission did not allow it. It
is replaced by two offline evaluations, each reported in
Section~\ref{sec:sop3-results}. The first is the date-based test of the
Gradient Boosting predictor on the historical cashiering log
(Section~\ref{sec:ml-training-protocol}). The second uses 166
transactions recorded by manual observation at Windows~3, 4, and~5 on
8, 9, and 22 September 2026. For each transaction the observer recorded
the ticket number, the time the ticket was issued, the time service
started, the time it ended, and the serving window. Waiting time is the
interval from ticket issuance to the start of service. The consequence
for interpretation is direct: the results describe how accurately wait
time can be predicted from the information the system uses, measured on
real observed waits, but they are not measurements of the deployed
system's live output, and no result is reported per prediction horizon.

\section{Research Instrument}
\label{sec:instrument}

Five instruments provide the evidence for the study: the \sysname{}
operational log, the manual observation record of cashier transactions,
a researcher-made baseline questionnaire, a researcher-made Likert
questionnaire for the user evaluation, and its revision for the user
acceptance test. The operational log and
observation record are described with the system performance results in
Section~\ref{sec:sop2-results}.

\textbf{Baseline questionnaire.} A separate researcher-made
questionnaire addresses Statement of the Problem No.~1 and describes
the current process. It exists in two forms against a shared five-point
scale, because the two groups experience the queue from opposite sides:
the staff form asks about running the queue, and the student form about
waiting in it. Each form contains fourteen problem statements in two
sections --- seven on perceived problems with wait-time and seven on
perceived problems with user experience. Every problem statement is
worded as a problem, so agreement indicates that the problem is present;
only the agreement half of Table~\ref{tab:likert} is applied to them,
since its quality half inverts the meaning of a problem statement. The
staff form ends with an open-ended question on the biggest problem. The
revised student form adds a screening question, questions on how the
student currently gets a number and learns of their turn, how long they
wait, and what the current system shows; four favourably worded
statements on the current display and announcements, interpreted with
the quality half of Table~\ref{tab:likert}; and choice questions on the
most frustrating part of the process, the features they would like, and
face registration. Its
internal consistency is tested with Cronbach's alpha computed on all
respondents as one group, students and staff together, for each section
and for the whole questionnaire, and the coefficients are reported with
the results in Chapter~3. The staff form also records tenure and how often the respondent handles the
queue; the student form records year level, frequency of use, and
familiarity with mobile applications and kiosks. The
full instrument appears in Appendix~\ref{app:sop1-questionnaire}.

\textbf{Likert-scale questionnaire.} A second questionnaire is
structured into three dimensions that correspond directly to Statement
of the Problem No.~4 in Chapter~1: (a)~system's usability, covering the
dashboard, mobile client, and ticket workflow; (b)~system's
reliability, covering queue assignment accuracy, face recognition,
and re-identification as perceived by users; and (c)~system clarity,
covering the dashboard layout, live snapshot, analytics cards, counter
controls, and the presentation of wait-time predictions. Each respondent
answers fifteen items, five per dimension: two common to all groups, so
that the groups can be compared, and three specific to their role. The
items are rated on the five-point scale shown in Table~\ref{tab:likert}
and listed in Appendix~\ref{app:queueflow-questionnaire}.

\textbf{Instrument validation.} The original design called for the
content validity of the researcher-made questionnaire to be established
by three expert raters before use, with the Content Validity Index
computed at the item level (I-CVI) and the scale level (S-CVI/Average).
Under the shortened evaluation protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}, this step was not carried out, and no
Content Validity Index is reported. The questionnaire's validity rests
instead on the construction of its items: each belongs to one of the
three dimensions named in Statement of the Problem No.~4, and each
role-specific item refers to a feature the respondent actually used
during the trial. Its reliability is measured on the responses.

\textbf{Reliability testing.} Internal-consistency reliability for the
user-evaluation questionnaire is computed with Cronbach's alpha, using
the formula in Section~2.7, on the six items worded identically in the
student and staff forms. The responses are pooled across the groups,
with two common items per dimension and all six together; a value of at
least 0.70 is taken as acceptable. The nine role-specific items in each
form are not treated as common items. The original design called for a separate
pilot of 10 to 15 respondents excluded from the final sample; under the
shortened evaluation protocol, alpha is instead computed on the
evaluation responses themselves, as it was for the baseline
questionnaire. A common-item scale that falls below 0.70 is reported with its
coefficient and interpreted item by item rather than as a composite. The
coefficients are reported with the results, in
Tables~\ref{tab:sop1-reliability} and~\ref{tab:sop4-reliability}.

\textbf{User acceptance test questionnaire.} The UAT uses a revision of
the student form of the user-evaluation questionnaire, listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:uat}. It keeps the three dimensions, five statements
each, and the five-point scale of Table~\ref{tab:likert}, but every
statement now names a step of the completed system --- registering the
face, joining the queue, signing in with Gbox, the \emph{Ahead of you}
count, the estimated against the actual wait, and deleting one's data
--- so that a student judges the step just performed rather than the
system in general. It also asks how the student received the queue
number, whether the system ever failed to recognise a student who had
joined, whether it ever gave a number to the wrong person, and how
comfortable the student is with being identified by face, and it ends
with two open-ended questions. Because its statements differ from those
of the September questionnaire, its results are reported separately and
are not pooled with those of Statement of the Problem No.~4.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

Ethical handling is treated as part of the system design, not only as a
research requirement. Staff and student users may join
surveys and system testing only after giving informed consent. Before
any questionnaire is administered, the researchers explain the purpose of the study, the activities involved,
the expected time commitment, possible benefits, and the participant's
right to withdraw without penalty.

Only respondents who are 18 years of age or older are enrolled. The
age criterion is enforced through a screening question that immediately
precedes the consent form, and any prospective respondent indicating an
age below 18 is not enrolled. No data from minors is collected or
stored at any point in the study.

For confidentiality, personal identifiers are removed before analysis.
Questionnaires do not collect names. Results are reported in aggregate
form so that individual respondents are not identifiable. Digital study
records are kept in password-protected storage accessible only to the
researchers and the thesis adviser, while hard-copy materials are placed
in locked storage. Non-biometric study records are kept for a maximum
of three years after completion and are then destroyed; hard copies are
shredded. How long the system itself keeps student accounts and face
data is set out under \emph{Data Retention} below.

\textbf{Camera Snapshot Handling.} The system captures live video
frames from the queue zone solely for automated person detection and
the live staff dashboard. The detector encodes annotated snapshots,
showing the camera frame with queue zone, person bounding boxes, and
queue numbers overlaid, at a configurable frame rate and transmits them
to the backend through an authenticated endpoint. The backend retains
only the latest snapshot in process memory; each new snapshot
overwrites the previous one, and no historical snapshot archive is
maintained on disk. Where an optional Redis cache is enabled in cloud
deployment, it is configured as a volatile cache only: snapshot keys
carry a short time-to-live, with a default of ten seconds, and Redis
persistence, including RDB snapshotting and AOF append-only logging, is
disabled in the bundled Redis configuration
(\texttt{save ""; appendonly no}). If a managed Redis provider is used,
persistence and provider-side backups for the \sysname{} cache instance
must be disabled, or the existence of provider-side persistence must be
disclosed in the deployment notes. Detection metadata, including person
tracks, queue length, and wait-time predictions, is transmitted through
a separate endpoint and is the only data persisted in the MySQL
database; the snapshot image itself is not written to MySQL at any
point. The queue-zone camera does perform face recognition on enrolled
students, and the handling of that biometric data is set out separately
below.

\textbf{Biometric Data and Face Enrolment.} Following the panel-mandated
revision, \sysname{} identifies enrolled students by face rather than by
clothing appearance. This constitutes processing of sensitive personal
information under Section~3(l) of the Data Privacy Act of 2012, and it is
treated as such.

Enrolment is self-service and requires two deliberate acts. A student
signs in with an institutional Gbox account and then completes a separate
face-capture step; neither alone enrols anyone. The capture submits one to
five photographs taken at varied angle and lighting, from which the system
computes a single averaged 512-dimensional ArcFace embedding. The
photographs themselves are never written to storage: they are read into
memory, converted to the embedding, and discarded within the same request.
No table in the database holds a face image, and the embedding is a
one-way numeric representation from which the original face cannot be
reconstructed.

\textbf{Consent Before Registration.}\label{cr:consent} No account exists until the student
agrees to a data-privacy notice. The first time a student signs in with a
Gbox account, the mobile client shows a \emph{Data Privacy Notice and
Consent} screen before asking for the student ID. The notice states what
is collected (the name and Gbox email from Google sign-in, the student ID
number, and face photographs, from which a face signature is computed
and the photographs discarded at once, together with the record of the
student's queue numbers), why it is collected (to recognise the student
at the cashier, to issue a number only after the student taps \emph{Get a
Queue Number}, and to show the student's position and estimated wait),
who may access it (authorised cashier staff and the system administrator,
with no third-party sharing or marketing), how long it is kept (the
retention policy below), and the student's rights under Republic Act
No.~10173, including withdrawing consent and deleting the account from
the Profile screen. Registering is voluntary: a student who taps
\emph{Not now} is told that the printed kiosk ticket remains available,
and no account is created. When the student taps \emph{I agree}, the
backend records the version of the notice that was accepted together with
the time. The backend, not only the app, enforces this: it refuses to
create an account, enrol a face, or issue a queue number for an account
without a recorded consent. Accounts created before the notice existed
are asked to accept it at their next sign-in and cannot request a number
or enrol a face until they do. This notice is separate from the
questionnaire consent form used in the evaluation.

That embedding, held in \texttt{student\_profiles}, is the only biometric
artefact the system retains. Embeddings computed at the queue-zone camera
during operation exist in process memory for the lifetime of a person's
track and are never written to the database; the recognition audit table
records only the matched student identifier, the similarity score, the
margin over the runner-up, and the accept-or-reject outcome.

\textbf{Data Retention.}\label{cr:retention} Students graduate or stop using the service, and
their records, above all their face data, should not stay in the database
indefinitely. \sysname{} applies a two-stage retention policy, and a
background process in the backend checks every account once a day.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Archive.} An account with no sign-in and no queue request
        for one year is archived: it is deactivated and its face
        signature is erased.
  \item \textbf{Delete.} An account that has stayed archived for one more
        year is deleted. Its past queue records are kept for the
        office's service statistics with the link to the student
        removed.
\end{enumerate}
A student who signs in while archived is restored and must enrol a face
again before being recognised. Staff can apply either stage to any
account at any time from the dashboard's Data Retention page, for example
to a batch of graduating students, so that graduates do not have to wait
out the year. Both periods are backend configuration values
(\texttt{RETENTION\_ARCHIVE\_AFTER\_DAYS} and
\texttt{RETENTION\_DELETE\_AFTER\_DAYS}, 365 days each) and are stated in
the privacy notice the student agrees to. De-identified survey responses
and aggregate performance results gathered for this study follow the
separate three-year limit stated above.

Recognition is not treated as consent to be queued. A recognized student
is issued a number only if they have separately requested one in the
mobile client, and that request lapses after a configurable interval. A
registered student who merely walks past the camera is marked as a
bystander and is issued nothing. People who are not enrolled are never
identified: acceptance requires both an absolute similarity threshold and
a margin over the next-best candidate, and the matcher refuses rather than
guesses when either condition fails.

Withdrawal is supported. A student can delete their own account from the
Profile screen of the mobile client at any time, which removes the
profile and its face signature from the database at once instead of
waiting for the retention policy. A student who registers again goes
through the consent notice and the face-capture step anew; the previous
embedding is not reused.

\textbf{Access Control and Transparency.} Dashboard access is
restricted to authenticated staff through session login, with JWT-based
camera-token validation between the detector and the backend. The
student-facing mobile client exposes only the requesting student's own
queue status, reached either through the signed-in student's own session
or through the ticket QR code and short-code credential issued to that
student; it does not display the camera feed or any other student's
status. Deployment requires physical signage at the service-office
entrance informing visitors that the area is monitored by an automated
queue management system, that enrolled students may be identified while
unenrolled visitors are detected without identification, and that face
enrolment is voluntary and may be withdrawn. The signage summarises the
retention policy, cites the Data Privacy Act of 2012, or Republic Act
No.~10173, and gives a contact for withdrawal and data-subject requests.
The in-app privacy notice gives the same information in full before
registration. The camera is
mounted visibly, not concealed. Consistent with the proportionality
principle of the Act, only the minimum data necessary for queue
management is processed; no marketing, profiling, or third-party data
sharing is performed.

The work is aligned with the Data Privacy Act of 2012, or Republic Act
No.~10173, and with applicable NCF research ethics protocols.
Deployment and data collection are subject to ethical clearance from
the host institution. Sources are cited and acknowledged
throughout the manuscript. Any use of AI-assisted tools is limited to
draft organization, language checking, or proofreading; the system
implementation, analysis, interpretation, and final responsibility for
the manuscript remain with the researchers.

\section{Statistical Treatment of Data}

The statistical treatment is organized around the questions raised in
Chapter~1. Technical measures are used for the detector, ticket, and
prediction components, while descriptive statistics are used for the
respondent ratings and profile data.

\textbf{Queue Assignment Accuracy and Re-Identification Reliability.}
These two metrics address Statement of the Problem No.~2. Queue
assignment accuracy is computed as the proportion of correctly assigned
queue numbers, matching the manual ground-truth log, over the total
number of arrivals observed during the evaluation period.
Re-identification reliability is computed as the proportion of correct
re-entry decisions, either correctly restoring a previous queue number
or correctly assigning a new one, over the total number of re-entry
events observed:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Accuracy} =
  \frac{\text{correct decisions}}{\text{total decisions}} \times 100\%
  \label{eq:accuracy}
\end{equation}
Both measures require the independent observer's ground-truth log,
which was not kept (Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}). They are defined
here for the evaluation recommended in Chapter~4; Statement of the
Problem No.~2 is answered instead by the offline recognition measures
below and by the system's own record of recognition decisions and
issued numbers.

\textbf{Face-Recognition Accuracy.} Recognition is additionally
evaluated offline on photographs whose identities are known, so that its
accuracy can be measured against ground truth rather than inferred from
the system's own log. Each held-out probe is compared with every
enrolled identity. A comparison between a probe and its own identity is
a \emph{genuine} pair; any other comparison is an \emph{impostor} pair.
At a match threshold $\tau$, the False Accept Rate is the proportion of
impostor pairs that score at or above $\tau$, and the False Reject Rate
is the proportion of genuine pairs that score below it:
\begin{equation}
  \text{FAR}(\tau) =
  \frac{\left|\{\text{impostor pairs with } s \geq \tau\}\right|}
       {\left|\{\text{impostor pairs}\}\right|}, \qquad
  \text{FRR}(\tau) =
  \frac{\left|\{\text{genuine pairs with } s < \tau\}\right|}
       {\left|\{\text{genuine pairs}\}\right|}
  \label{eq:far-frr}
\end{equation}
The Equal Error Rate (EER) is the value at which the two rates coincide,
and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) is the probability that a
randomly chosen genuine pair outscores a randomly chosen impostor pair;
both describe the embedding model independently of any threshold. The
decision rule itself is then evaluated at the deployed thresholds as an
identification outcome for each probe --- correctly linked, linked to
the wrong identity, or refused and escalated to staff --- and, in an
open-set test, by removing each person from the gallery and probing
with their photographs, so that the rate at which a non-enrolled person
is wrongly identified can be measured directly.

\textbf{Mean Absolute Error and Mean Absolute Percentage Error.} These
two metrics address Statement of the Problem No.~3. The original design
computed them separately for the current, 5-minute, 15-minute, and
30-minute prediction horizons; under the change described in
Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}, they are computed on observed waits
without a per-horizon breakdown:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MAE} =
  \frac{1}{n}\sum |y_{\text{predicted}} - y_{\text{actual}}|
  \label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum\left|
    \frac{y_{\text{actual}} - y_{\text{predicted}}}{y_{\text{actual}}}
  \right| \times 100\%
  \label{eq:mape}
\end{equation}
where $y_{\text{predicted}}$ is the predicted wait and
$y_{\text{actual}}$ is the observed wait, from the issuance of the
ticket to the start of service, as recorded by manual observation.
Because MAPE divides by the actual wait, it is undefined for students
who waited zero minutes; such cases are excluded from MAPE only. The
Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE), which divides the total
absolute error by the total observed wait and therefore remains defined
when individual waits are zero, is reported alongside it:
\begin{equation}
  \text{WAPE} =
  \frac{\sum |y_{\text{actual}} - y_{\text{predicted}}|}
       {\sum y_{\text{actual}}} \times 100\%
  \label{eq:wape}
\end{equation}
Every predictor is compared with a constant baseline that predicts the
median wait of the training data for every student. A predictor is
considered useful only if its error is lower than this baseline's on
data it was not fitted to.

\textbf{Weighted Mean and Overall Weighted Mean.} These two measures
address Statements of the Problem No.~1 and No.~4. For No.~1 they are
computed separately for staff and students, whose baseline items
differ, and are interpreted on the agreement scale only. The weighted
mean (WM) is
computed for each Likert item, each dimension averaged across items
within a dimension, and each respondent group: cashier staff and
students. The results are then pooled into an
overall weighted mean (OWM). Per-group weighted means are reported to
allow comparison across stakeholder perspectives; because staff and
students answer different role-specific items for No.~4, groups are
compared on the common items only.
\begin{equation}
  \text{WM} = \frac{\sum(f \times w)}{N}
  \label{eq:weighted_mean}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the frequency of each response, $w$ is the weight assigned
to that response from 1 to 5 on the Likert scale, and $N$ is the number
of respondents in the group.
\begin{equation}
  \text{OWM} = \frac{\sum \text{WM}}{n}
  \label{eq:overall_weighted_mean}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of items per dimension or the number of
dimensions per group, depending on which level of aggregation is being
computed. Interpretation follows the verbal categories in
Table~\ref{tab:likert}.

\textbf{Percentage Distribution.} Percentage is used to describe the
composition of the respondent sample by group, year level, role, and
demographic profile, and the distribution of responses across the
five-point scale for each item:
\begin{equation}
  P = \frac{f}{N} \times 100
  \label{eq:percentage}
\end{equation}

\textbf{User Acceptance Test.} The UAT ratings are summarised with the
weighted mean (Equation~\ref{eq:weighted_mean}) for each statement, each
dimension, and overall, and interpreted with Table~\ref{tab:likert}.
The answers on how the number was received, on recognition failures and
wrong numbers, and on comfort with face identification are reported as
frequencies and percentages (Equation~\ref{eq:percentage}), as are the
counts from the system's log: face signatures saved and face sign-ins
accepted. Internal consistency is measured with Cronbach's alpha
computed on all UAT respondents as one group, for each dimension and for
all fifteen statements.

\textbf{Cronbach's Alpha.} Cronbach's alpha is used to assess the
internal-consistency reliability of each researcher-made questionnaire.
It is computed on all respondents of a questionnaire as one group,
students and cashier staff together, rather than separately for each
group:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha =
  \frac{k}{k - 1}
  \left(1 - \frac{\sum\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_{\text{total}}^2}\right)
  \label{eq:cronbach_alpha}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the number of items, $\sigma_i^2$ is the variance of each
item, and $\sigma_{\text{total}}^2$ is the variance of the total score.
Values of 0.70 or higher are taken as acceptable.

\textbf{Detection-to-Ticket Latency.} Detection-to-ticket latency is
reported as the mean and standard deviation, in seconds, of the elapsed
time from the first confirmed detection of a queue entrant to the
issuance of the corresponding ticket payload, computed from the
automated operational log.

\section{System Design and Architecture}
\label{sec:design-arch}

\sysname{} is designed as a set of cooperating components rather than as
a single monolithic application. The detector, re-identification logic,
ticket workflow, prediction module, dashboard, FastAPI backend, and
MySQL data layer are developed and tested iteratively so that changes
in one component can be checked against the behavior of the rest of the
prototype.

\subsection{Design Principles}

\textbf{Recognition Accuracy.} The system must never issue an enrolled
student's queue number to someone else. Person detection establishes
only that someone is present; a queue number is issued only when the
face-based identity validation accepts an enrolled student under the
threshold-and-margin rule, and anyone the rule cannot identify with
confidence is referred to staff rather than guessed at. Recognition
accuracy, the rate of wrong identities, and the rejection of
non-enrolled people are the primary quality gates, reported in
Chapter~3.

\textbf{Prediction Usefulness.} The prediction module must produce
wait-time estimates whose MAE and MAPE, at each of the current,
5-minute, 15-minute, and 30-minute horizons, are low enough to be
actionable for staff planning. Specific target thresholds are reported
in Chapter~3.

\textbf{API Reliability.} The REST endpoints must handle malformed,
empty, or unusually long inputs gracefully, returning structured error
responses rather than crashing. Endpoint behavior must be consistent
and deterministic across repeated calls with identical inputs, and
inference time must remain within bounded latency for live use.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All model configurations, queue-zone
parameters, prediction smoothing factors, and confirmation-window
values used in the prototype are recorded as configurable defaults in
version-controlled code stored in the project repository, ensuring
results reported in this study can be independently verified.

\textbf{Privacy Protection.} Consistent with the visual privacy
literature reviewed in Chapter~1, the system stores derived data rather
than imagery. Detection produces bounding boxes, and identification
produces face embeddings, which are one-way numeric representations from
which a face cannot be reconstructed. No photograph, video segment or
camera frame is written to the database at any point, and enrolment
photographs are discarded once their embedding has been computed. Because
the embedding is nonetheless biometric data, its handling is governed by
the safeguards set out in Section~\ref{sec:ethics}.

\textbf{Presentation Layer.}\label{cr:presentation} This layer has three user-facing surfaces.

otherwise from the backend's authenticated stream, alerts for people the system could not

prediction-accuracy panel that compares each estimate with the real
wait, a download of the service records as CSV, and the Data Retention
page. The \emph{student mobile application}, built in Flutter, opens on a
\sysname{} loading screen and handles sign-in with the student's NCF Gbox
account or face, the data-privacy consent, voluntary face enrolment, and
the request for a number. The request is made by tapping \emph{Get a
Queue Number} and filling in a payment-slip form laid out like the
cashier's paper Payment Slip (Tuition, Books, Special Exam with the
subject, and Others with a description, each with an amount, and a
TOTAL that adds itself up), then tapping \emph{Request Number}. The
application then shows the student's number, the people ahead, the
estimated wait, the windows open, and the numbers being served; warns
before a request when the wait would be 15 minutes or more; and changes
to \emph{It's your turn} with the window number when the student is
called, or to a notice when staff marked the number as skipped. It never
shows the camera feed or any other student's status. Its wording follows
one term for each step --- \emph{Get a Queue Number}, \emph{Request
Number}, \emph{Face check}, \emph{Your number} --- and \emph{window}
throughout, following the TRC review. The \emph{public display board}
renders the active queue in the waiting area and announces each call
using the browser's built-in Web Speech API, so no audio leaves the
browser.

\textbf{API Layer.} This layer is a FastAPI application hosted on Render
and is organised into seven routers. \texttt{auth.py} handles staff
sign-in and sessions; \texttt{students.py} handles student sign-in, the
consent record, face enrolment, the payment-slip lines, the request for
a number, and a student's own queue status; \texttt{detector\_api.py}
receives detection metadata and snapshots from the detector;
\texttt{queue.py} serves the queue list (staff only), the public display
data, queue status, prediction, analytics, prediction accuracy, the
service-record CSV, and the staff actions of taking a window, calling the
next number, switching the calling mode, and marking an entry done or a
no-show; \texttt{crowd.py} serves the staff-only video stream, snapshots,
and recent history; \texttt{retention.py} serves the retention policy
and the staff archive and delete actions; and \texttt{health.py} reports
the state of the backend, database, cache, and snapshot feed. The
backend runs on Render's paid 0.5-CPU instance, which does not sleep
when idle.

\textbf{Computer Vision Layer.} This layer is a separate Python process
running on the office computer attached to the camera, implemented in
\texttt{app/detector.py}. It reads frames through OpenCV, runs YOLOv8n with
ByteTrack~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack} persistent tracking, and passes each
detection through five quality gates --- minimum bounding-box area,
maximum frame coverage, portrait aspect ratio, motion energy, and
non-maximum suppression --- before smoothing its box with an exponential
moving average. For each tracked person whose face is visible, it then
computes the face embedding locally with InsightFace \texttt{buffalo\_s}.
Only the resulting metadata --- tracks, boxes, and embeddings --- and a
compressed snapshot for the live view are sent to the backend, over
requests authenticated by a camera token. Running the detector as its
own process keeps camera work from blocking the dashboard or the API.
Gate thresholds and the smoothing factor are specified in
Section~\ref{subsec:cv-layer}.

\textbf{Business Logic Layer.} This layer is implemented in
\texttt{app/services/} and runs on Render with the API. The \emph{Queue
Service} removes weak detections, applies the centroid-in-rectangle zone
test (\S\ref{subsec:candidate}), and passes confirmed detections to the
Queue Tracker. The \emph{Queue Tracker} holds the active queue in memory;
confirms presence through a 14-update buffer with a motion check, or,
once the detector has captured the person's face, after one second in
the zone and three updates with a face, so that confirmation does not
slow down when the detector reaches the cloud backend only two to three
times a second (\S\ref{subsec:candidate}); keeps tracks stable through identifier
remapping (\S\ref{subsec:remap}) and duplicate suppression with re-entry
immunity (\S\ref{subsec:dedup}); and, through the \emph{Face Service},
compares each confirmed person's embedding with the enrolled roster under
the threshold-and-margin rule (\S\ref{subsec:reid}). A queue number is
issued only to an enrolled student who is recognised with confidence
and has asked for a number in the mobile application; anyone else stays
unlinked, and after 45~seconds the dashboard alerts staff
(Figure~\ref{fig:detection-ticket-pipeline}). Walk-ins are not handled
here at all: they take a ticket from the office's existing kiosk, which
runs separately from \sysname{}. When a number is issued, the payment
slip the student filled in is attached to it, so the cashier sees what
the student is paying before the student reaches the window.

The Face Service keeps every enrolled embedding in one in-memory matrix
and scores a live face against all of them in a single matrix--vector
product, instead of reading each embedding from the database and
comparing it in a loop. The matrix is rebuilt only when an enrolment
changes or after a time limit. At 10,000 enrolled students it occupies
about 20~MB and a match takes about 3~ms
(Section~\ref{subsec:load-test}).

\textbf{Emergency manual entry.} The dashboard also gives staff a manual
override for the occasions when automatic recognition cannot do its job:
the camera is blocked or offline, the detector has stopped, or a
registered student is standing in the queue but cannot be recognised,
for example because of poor lighting or a face covering. Staff can use
it in two ways. A person the camera sees but could not identify, shown
among the pending-link alerts, can be given a queue number directly; and
when the camera cannot see the person at all, staff can type a queue
number to create the entry by hand. A manual entry joins the queue in the
same order as any other, receives a counter and a ticket in the usual
way, and stays in the queue until staff mark it done or a no-show, since
there is no camera track that could expire. The system refuses any
number already issued that day, so manual entry cannot create a
duplicate. This is an emergency feature, not part of the normal flow, and
every entry made through it is recorded as manual; how often staff
needed it is therefore itself evidence of how often automatic
recognition fell short. Because the dashboard runs in the cloud, the
override covers a failure of the camera or of recognition, but not a loss
of the office's internet connection.

The \emph{Prediction Service} estimates each student's wait by simulating
the live line window by window. Each window is free now, free when its
current transaction is expected to end, or closed until a time staff set
(for example, lunch); the people still waiting are taken in queue order,
each by the window that frees up first, which is then busy for one
service time. The moment a student is taken is the start of their turn,
and the wait is that moment minus the present time. A transaction that
has already run past its usual length is given three quarters of a
service time to go, from the cashier log. The service time is supplied by
the dynamic service-time measurement described in
Section~\ref{subsec:service-time}, which recomputes it every five minutes
from the transaction log, starting from the 2.0-minute median of the
observed transactions. For the student's own wait, this estimator
replaced the M/M/c baseline of \S\ref{subsubsec:mmc-baseline}; the
trend, Holt, and growth-ratio forecasts of
\S\ref{subsubsec:short-forecast}, \S\ref{subsubsec:holt-forecast}, and
\S\ref{subsubsec:long-forecast} remain on the analytics view for the
crowd as a whole. It was chosen because it uses what the system knows
exactly at the moment: the order
of the line, who is at which window and since when, and which windows
are open. The predictor calibrated on the number of people ahead
(Section~\ref{sec:sop3-results}) is kept as its benchmark and as a
fallback that can be switched on without changing the code. Every issued
number records the estimate the student was shown, the people ahead, and
the windows open, and the time it was called, so the dashboard scores the
estimator against real waits as numbers are served.

\textbf{Calling the next number.}\label{cr:calling} By default, numbers are called by
staff. Each staff account takes one cashier window, and only one account
can hold a window at a time; the claim is renewed while the dashboard is
open and lapses two minutes after it closes. When a cashier is free, they
press \emph{Call next}, and the first waiting number is assigned to their
window; the student's application then changes to \emph{It's your turn}
with the window number, and the display board announces the call. The
window stays with that number until the cashier marks it done or a
no-show, and the window is recorded with the transaction. A switch on
the dashboard, protected by a confirmation step, changes to automatic
calling, in which the \emph{Counter Service} assigns windows itself with
the sticky-assignment rule: the active queue is sorted by queue number,
and each unassigned person at a position no greater than the number of
open windows is given the smallest free window
(\S\ref{subsec:counters}). The setting survives a reset of the queue.

The \emph{Ticket Service} is a background worker that consumes
ticket-generation jobs. The \emph{Ticket Printer} produces the short
code, the JSON Web Token signed with HMAC-SHA256, the QR-encoded status
URL, and the PDF ticket, which is stored in object storage; in the
deployment configuration it drives a thermal printer instead. The short
code is drawn from a 32-character alphabet that excludes the ambiguous
characters 0, O, 1, and I, and is formatted as \texttt{XXXX--XXXX}. The
JWT carries a four-hour expiry and a 32-hex-digit nonce to prevent
replay. The complete payload specification is in
Section~\ref{subsec:ticket}.

\textbf{Persistence Layer.} This layer is a managed MySQL database hosted
on Aiven, accessed through \texttt{app/database/database\_handler.py},
together with S3-compatible object storage on Supabase for PDF tickets.
The database holds student profiles and their face embeddings, queue
records with their short codes, tokens, statuses, and timestamps,
operational events, and the audit of every recognition decision. Raw
camera frames, live snapshots, enrolment photographs, and the in-memory
queue state are intentionally never persisted. The student profile and
its embedding are governed by the retention policy in
Section~\ref{sec:ethics}: the embedding is erased when the account is
archived and the profile is deleted a year later. Queue records and
recognition audits never hold the embedding, and the database also
records which version of the privacy notice each student accepted.

\textbf{Cache and Runtime State Layer.} This layer comprises an in-memory
state module, \texttt{app/state.py}, holding the latest detector output,
the latest annotated snapshot, and a short rolling history, and a managed
Redis cache on Upstash, accessed through
\texttt{app/services/cache\_service.py}, which shares live values
between backend workers. The cache is volatile by design: every key
carries a short time-to-live and nothing in it is written to disk as a
record of the queue.

\subsection{Major Algorithms}
\label{subsec:major-algorithms}
\label{cr:algorithms}

Four algorithms carry the system's detection, recognition, and
prediction. Each is evaluated in Chapter~3 and specified in full, with
its equations, parameter values, and numbered steps, in
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Algorithm 1: Person Detection and Tracking.} YOLOv8n
        detects people in each camera frame and ByteTrack keeps an
        identifier on each. Five quality gates --- minimum box area,
        maximum frame coverage, portrait shape, motion energy, and
        non-maximum suppression --- reject false detections, an
        exponential moving average smooths each box, and a
        track-management step reattaches a person who briefly leaves the
        frame and removes duplicate tracks.
  \item \textbf{Algorithm 2: Presence Confirmation.} A person counts as
        standing in the queue when the centre of their box stays inside
        the marked zone for 14 updates and passes a motion test, or,
        once the detector has captured their face, for one second with
        three updates carrying a face.
  \item \textbf{Algorithm 3: Face-Based Identity Validation and Number
        Issuance.} The detector computes a 512-number ArcFace embedding
        of the face, and the backend compares it with every enrolled
        student. A match is accepted only if the best cosine similarity
        is at least 0.30 \emph{and} leads the runner-up by at least
        0.15. A recognised student who has requested a number receives
        one from the system's range starting at 5000; anyone not
        recognised with confidence is referred to staff.
  \item \textbf{Algorithm 4: Wait-Time Estimation.} The wait shown to
        each student is computed by simulating the line window by
        window from the order of the line, the windows open, and the
        measured service time. A regression on the number of people
        ahead is its benchmark and fallback, and each estimate is logged
        against the real wait.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Calling the next number to a window, automatic window
assignment, and ticket generation apply fixed rules and are given in the
appendix as supporting procedures.

\subsection{Dynamic Service-Time Measurement}
\label{sec:dynamic-service-time-method}

\sysname{} treats average service time as a value that can change
during live operation, not as a fixed assumption decided before
deployment. Every five minutes, a background measurement routine reads
recent completed transactions from the MySQL log and estimates the
current service pace from inter-departure gaps. For completed
transaction timestamps $t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_N$, each gap is computed in
minutes as

\begin{equation}
g_i = \frac{t_i - t_{i-1}}{60}, \qquad i = 2, \ldots, N.
\label{eq:method-svc-gap}
\end{equation}

\noindent Gaps below 0.1 minute are treated as database-write noise,
while gaps above 15 minutes are treated as idle periods rather than
normal cashier service. The retained gaps are averaged and multiplied
by the number of active counters to account for parallel service. The
new estimate is then blended with the previously stored value:

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{svc}}_{\text{new}} =
0.7\,\widehat{\mathrm{svc}} +
0.3\,\widehat{\mathrm{svc}}_{\text{prev}}.
\label{eq:method-svc-blend}
\end{equation}

\noindent This dynamic measurement supplies the service rate $\mu$ used
by the prediction module after the system has recorded enough
transactions. The pre-deployment service-time calibration in
Section~\ref{sec:datagathering} is therefore used only as the initial
seed value.

\subsection{Machine-Learning Wait-Time Predictor Training Protocol}
\label{sec:ml-training-protocol}

\sysname{} does not train its wait-time model while the cashiering
prototype is already serving users. Training is handled as an offline
step: the researchers prepare the transaction logs, test several
predictors on past records, and deploy a model only if it is more
accurate than simpler alternatives. The Gradient Boosting predictor
described in this subsection was trained and evaluated in this way, and
was not deployed (see \emph{Integration Plan and Outcome} below). This keeps experimental
model changes away from live queue operations and gives the researchers
a reproducible basis for the results presented later in the manuscript.

\textbf{Dataset.} The working dataset comes from historical cashiering
queue logs made available for \sysname{} predictor development. The XML
export contains 49,303 queue records covering March~24,~2023 to
May~4,~2026. The available fields include queue identifier, application
identifier, queue number, service date, service time, transaction type,
processing status, and program identifier. Names, student numbers,
payment amounts, account details, and other personally identifiable
fields are not used by the model. Records marked with
\texttt{processing = 3} are excluded because they represent no-show,
cancelled, or incomplete queue events.

\textbf{Feature Engineering.} The predictor uses three feature groups.
The first group is derived directly from the queue logs and timestamp
sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Queue and transaction fields: queue number, transaction type,
        program identifier, daily queue size, and estimated people
        ahead.
  \item Time fields: hour, minute, day of week, month, morning or
        afternoon indicator, and peak-hour indicator.
  \item Recent-demand fields: arrivals within the previous 5 and
        15 minutes.
\end{enumerate}
The second group consists of academic-period indicators for enrollment,
prelim, midterm, final, and clearance periods. These indicators allow
the model to distinguish ordinary service days from predictable
cashiering-demand spikes.

The third group grounds the AI model in the analytical prediction
framework of \sysname{}. For every training row, the preparation script
computes outputs from the mathematical routines described in
Section~\ref{subsec:prediction}: an M/M/c-style baseline wait, a
local trend-projected wait, a Holt double-exponential smoothing
estimate, a growth-ratio mean-reversion estimate, the estimated
arrival rate, and the estimated service time. These analytical outputs
are used as input features to the machine-learning model rather than
as replacements for it.

The response variable is reconstructed wait time in minutes. Because
the XML export does not contain both a queue-entry timestamp and a
counter-arrival timestamp for every record, wait time is reconstructed
as the elapsed time from a valid queue record to the next valid served
record on the same service date. Reconstructed waits below one minute
are excluded during model fitting because sub-minute gaps may represent
immediate transaction encoding or log artifacts rather than meaningful
queue waiting. Reconstructed waits above 120 minutes are excluded to
avoid idle-period outliers.

\textbf{Train and Test Split.} Queue records are split by service date,
not by random row assignment. The first 80\% of ordered service dates
form the training set and the remaining 20\% form the test set. This
order matters because cashiering demand is time-dependent: enrollment
week, payment deadlines, and ordinary class days do not behave the same
way. A random split could allow future queue patterns to influence
model tuning, so it is avoided.

\textbf{Model Architecture.} The machine-learning predictor trained in
this study uses Gradient Boosting Regression through scikit-learn's
\texttt{HistGradientBoostingRegressor}. Gradient boosting is selected
because the available data is tabular and contains mixed operational,
calendar, and analytical prediction features. The model learns
nonlinear corrections from historical queue behavior while still being
anchored to the interpretable mathematical estimates computed by the
prediction service.

Deep sequence models such as Long Short-Term Memory networks and the
Temporal Fusion Transformer are not used in this version of the
prototype. These architectures may be useful for large, multi-office
time-series datasets, but the available cashiering data comes from a
single service office. For this study, simpler tabular models are more
appropriate and easier to validate.

\textbf{Hyperparameter Selection.} The prototype model uses 300
boosting iterations, a learning rate of 0.05, 31 maximum leaf nodes,
and L2 regularization of 0.1. These conservative settings were chosen
to keep the model stable on the reconstructed wait-time target and to
avoid overfitting to short timestamp gaps.

\textbf{Evaluation Protocol.} The selected model is evaluated on the
date-based held-out test set using MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and $R^2$. MAE and
RMSE are treated as the primary metrics because reconstructed waits can
be short, making percentage-based error unstable. MAPE is still
reported for transparency but is interpreted cautiously.

\textbf{Integration Plan and Outcome.} The plan was to serialize the
trained model with \texttt{joblib}, load it in the FastAPI backend at
startup, compute the live feature vector for each status request, and
calibrate the Gradient Boosting output with the current day's service
speed, the previous day's wait at the same hour, and recent prediction
error before showing it. This was not carried out, for three reasons.
First, on the 166 observed transactions
(Section~\ref{subsec:sop3-offline}), a simpler predictor calibrated on
the number of people ahead was more accurate than the learned
alternatives that could be tested there; the Gradient Boosting predictor
itself could be tested only on the historical log, so the two results
are not directly comparable. Second, the runtime calibration layer was
not implemented. Third, loading the scikit-learn and pandas libraries
would use about 100~MB of the backend's 512~MB of memory. The deployed
system therefore estimates the wait by simulating the line window by
window (Algorithm~4, Section~\ref{subsec:prediction}) and keeps a
regression on the number of people ahead, stored as two plain
coefficients, as its benchmark and fallback. The trained Gradient
Boosting model remains a documented benchmark of this study.

\textbf{Retraining and Model Drift.} The procedure in this subsection
covers the initial training run only. Periodic retraining, automated
drift monitoring, and production retraining pipelines are outside the
present scope, although the prototype is arranged so that a later model
file can replace the original one. The resulting training metrics are
reported in Chapter~3.

\section{Software Development Process}
\label{sec:sdp}

\sysname{} development is organized as a small Agile-style engineering
cycle. Requirements, interface decisions, coding work, testing, and
review are repeated across modules instead of being handled as one
large build at the end of the study.

\textbf{Planning and Requirement Analysis.} System requirements were
drawn from the baseline survey of students and staff and from
conversations with the cashier staff while the wait-time data were
being collected. The user needs, expected workflows, dashboard actions,
and ticket conventions identified in this way were used to prioritize
features and align development goals with the conceptual framework
presented in Chapter~1.

\textbf{System Design.} This phase focuses on the architecture and
design of the detection pipeline, the backend, the database, and the
dashboard. It includes queue-zone coordinate design, ticket-payload
schema design, prediction-module parameter design, and dashboard
wireframing.

\textbf{Development Iteration and Sprint Cycles.} Development is
organized into sprint cycles, each concentrating on a specific module.
The sprints, in the order in which they were executed, are
summarized below:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item YOLOv8n pipeline setup, queue-zone configuration, and the
        custom NMS pre-filter for duplicate head and body box
        suppression.
  \item Re-entry matching, first by clothing appearance and, after the
        pre-oral defense, replaced by self-service face enrolment and
        face-based identification with the threshold-and-margin decision
        rule, debugged iteratively with live camera input.
  \item Queue position recalculation logic.
  \item Prediction module implementation of the M/M/c baseline,
        short-term trend projection, Holt smoothing, and
        mean-reversion long forecast, tuned iteratively against logged
        wait times.
  \item FastAPI backend, staff web dashboard, and counter controls.
  \item Printer-agnostic ticket workflow, including JWT issuance,
        short-code generation, QR linking, and PDF rendering for
        demonstration.
  \item Student status integration, including mobile client, status
        API, and end-to-end ticket scan tests.
  \item Integration testing, performance tuning, and final
        pre-evaluation hardening.
\end{enumerate}

Each of the eight sprints listed above was executed through a five-phase
cycle: Plan, Design, Build, Test, and Review. Sprint-level Plan and
Design refine the module's acceptance criteria and interface contracts
before Build; Test validates the deliverable against those criteria;
and Review captures adviser feedback together with measured performance,
which then propagates into the Plan phase of the following sprint.
This per-sprint feedback loop instantiates, at the development level,
the Feedback stage described in the conceptual framework of
Section~\ref{sec:conceptual-framework}.

\section{Validation and Testing Methods}
\label{cr:validation}

Validation combines software tests, observed performance, and user
feedback. Functional and integration tests check whether the prototype
works as intended; recognition, issuance, prediction, and load measures
address the technical research questions; and the survey addresses user
evaluation.

\textbf{Alpha Testing.} Alpha testing was conducted internally by the
developers using live camera input, together with an automated test
suite that drives the student and cashier flows through the queue logic
without writing to the live database. No test footage was recorded, so
alpha-testing defects are not reported quantitatively. Defects in
detection, tracking, recognition, ticket generation, dashboard
rendering, and mobile-client status retrieval were identified and
resolved before external users were exposed to the system.

\textbf{Beta Testing.} Under the shortened evaluation protocol, beta
testing took the form of a supervised trial in which volunteer students
used the system on a test installation on the NCF campus, since the
system was not deployed at the cashiering office, and the cashier staff
were given the staff dashboard to use. The developers observed the
interaction, and the same trial, rather than a separate two-week period
of regular operation, provided the structured evaluation described
below.

\textbf{User Acceptance Testing.} The final check is whether the people
who would use \sysname{} accept the completed system, including the
features added after the pre-final defense: the consent step, the
payment-slip form, calling by window, the turn and skipped notices, and
the retention controls. In the UAT, students use the deployed system
and then rate each step on the UAT questionnaire
(Appendix~\ref{app:uat}), so that a step can be judged without knowing
how the system works inside, and the system's own log of the session
shows where a step failed. The UAT is facilitated by a
BS Information Systems student, and its results are evaluated by a
statistician (Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}, Phase~4).

\textbf{Performance Testing: Queue Assignment Accuracy and
Re-Identification Reliability.} The original design measured both
against an independent observer's manual ground-truth log of every
actual queue entrant and every re-entry event, using the formula in
Section~2.7. That log was not kept (Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}).
Recognition accuracy is instead measured offline on held-out
photographs of known identity, which provide the ground truth the live
record lacks, and the live results are taken from the system's own
record of recognition decisions and issued numbers
(Section~\ref{sec:sop2-results}).

\textbf{Performance Testing: Prediction MAE and MAPE.} The original
design timed actual waits with a stopwatch and compared them with the
system's predictions at each horizon. Under the change described in
Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}, MAE and MAPE are computed offline, on
the historical cashiering log and on the 166 observed cashier
transactions, using the formulas in Section~2.7.

\textbf{Performance Testing: Latency and Ticket Flow.} The time from a
person's arrival in the queue zone to an issued queue number is
measured from the timestamps the system logs at each step
(Section~\ref{subsec:sop2-issuance}). The detection-to-ticket latency
measured from the first detection that passes the quality gates of the
Computer Vision Layer (\S\ref{subsec:cv-layer}), and the ticket flow
validation, in which the short code, QR link, and JWT of each ticket are
checked to resolve to the matching queue status, were not measured
separately and are recommended for the evaluation in Chapter~4.

\textbf{Usability Evaluation.} Immediately after the supervised trial
session, the questionnaire in Section~2.5 is administered to the
respondent groups of Section~2.3. Its reliability, like that of the
baseline questionnaire (Section~\ref{sec:sop1-results}), is measured
with Cronbach's alpha, $0.70$ or more being acceptable (Section~2.5).
Responses are analysed with the procedures in Section~2.7, and the
open-ended answers are read for problems the ratings do not capture.

\textbf{Load Testing.} To assess whether the backend stays responsive
under concurrent use, a load test was conducted with Locust, simulating
simultaneous users polling the FastAPI backend: students checking their
queue status, the public display board, staff viewing analytics, and
staff logins. The metrics are response time under load and error rate
(Section~\ref{subsec:load-test}); recovery from overload was not tested.

This section addresses Statement of the Problem No.~1: \emph{What are

instrument described in Section~\ref{sec:instrument}.

Ninety respondents are analysed: 83 students and 7 staff members of the
service office. The staff answered the staff form from 16 to 23
September 2026. The students answered the student form, revised to add
questions on current queueing practice, the current display, and face
registration, on 3 October and from 12 to 16 November 2026. Because
\sysname{} was not deployed at the cashiering office, every respondent
describes the current kiosk-and-display process. Of 155 student
submissions, 72 were excluded before analysis: 50 identical submissions
made one second apart on 11 November, which could not have been
completed by separate respondents; 2 whose consent field did not record
agreement; and 20 from 3 October that gave the same eighteen ratings in
the same order and could not be confirmed as separate respondents.

Among the staff, 6 handle queue transactions daily and 4 have done so
for one to three years. Among the students, 45 ($54.2\%$) are in third
year, 22 ($26.5\%$) in second, 12 ($14.5\%$) in fourth, and 4 ($4.8\%$)
in first; 52 ($62.7\%$) queue for services daily or several times a
week; and 75 ($90.4\%$) are very familiar with mobile applications or
digital kiosks. These are regular users of the office, not first-time
visitors.

The two groups answered different problem statements against the same
five-point scale --- staff about running the queue, students about
waiting in it --- so the weighted means of each group are reported side
by side. Every problem statement is worded as a problem, so agreement
means the problem is real, and only the agreement half of
Table~\ref{tab:likert} is used for them; its quality half reads
backwards on a problem statement, where agreeing is the unfavourable
outcome.

Because the questionnaire is researcher-made, its internal consistency
was tested with Cronbach's alpha before the results were interpreted,
with all ninety respondents treated as one group
(Table~\ref{tab:sop1-reliability}). Both forms have the same structure:
seven statements on wait-time problems and seven on user-experience
problems, answered on the same agreement scale. For each respondent, the
seven statements of their form in a section were taken as that section's
seven items, so that students and staff contribute to the same two
section scales and to the fourteen-item whole questionnaire. The answers
were coded from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree); the sample
variance of each item was taken across the ninety respondents; each
respondent's item scores were summed and the sample variance of those
totals was taken; and the values were substituted into
Equation~\ref{eq:cronbach_alpha}. For the whole questionnaire, the sum of
the fourteen item variances was $7.728839$ and the variance of the total
scores was $50.949313$, giving
$\alpha = (14/13)(1 - 7.728839/50.949313) = 0.913557$, reported as
$0.914$.

All three scales are reliable: wait-time ($\alpha = 0.804$), user
experience ($\alpha = 0.942$), and the whole questionnaire
($\alpha = 0.914$). The four statements on the current display, which
only the student form asks, give $\alpha = 0.901$ across the 83
students. Because the statements at each position are worded for the
respondent's role, these coefficients describe the consistency of the
section scores across all respondents rather than of identical
statements.

\subsection{The Gap Between the Two Groups}
\label{subsec:sop1-gap}

The difference between $4.64$ and $3.34$ is the central finding of this
section, and it is not a weakness in the data. Staff and students were
asked about the same process and gave different answers because the
process costs them different things. The student bears the uncertainty:
standing within sight of a display, unable to estimate the wait,
occasionally missing a turn. The staff member does not --- from behind
the counter the queue is visible, its length is known, and the next
number is whatever the system prints.

This explains why the problem has persisted. A cost that falls entirely
on the people waiting is invisible to the people who would have to
authorise fixing it. It also sets the design priority for
\sysname{}: the features that address the highest-scoring student items
--- knowing the position in line, an estimated wait, and a notification
before the turn arrives --- are directed at the group that reported the
problem, not the group that administers it.

\subsection{The Problem Staff Named Instead}
\label{subsec:sop1-connectivity}

When the Likert items ended and staff were asked in their own words
what the biggest problem is, six of the seven gave the same answer, and
none of them named anything the Likert items had asked about:

\begin{quote}
``Internet connection.''\\
``No internet.''\\
``No internet connection during peak season, it cause long lines.''\\
``Glitch of queueing system when there's no internet, and running out
of paper tickets when peak or busy periods.''\\
``Glitch of the system, repetitive of number called by the system, no
internet which caused system failure, offline, no thermal paper
available.''\\
``Unreliable or slow internet connection compounds every problem in the
queue process\ldots{} you can't trust the status, and you waste time
redoing work.''
\end{quote}

\noindent This is the most consequential result in the baseline survey,
and it is reported here rather than set aside because it bears directly
on the deployed design of \sysname{}. The office already operates a
networked queueing system with a display and thermal tickets; what
staff experience as the daily failure is not the queue logic but the
connection underneath it.

\sysname{} is more exposed to this failure, not less. Its backend,
database, cache, and object storage are cloud-hosted, and the local
detector must reach the backend for every recognition and every queue
number issued. A connection loss that today costs the office its
display and its ticket printer would, under \sysname{}, additionally
stop face recognition and number issuance. The prototype was in fact
unreachable during one verification attempt in this study while general
connectivity was unaffected, which is the same failure mode the staff
describe.

Two consequences follow, and both are carried forward rather than
resolved here. First, the connectivity requirement is stated as a
delimitation in Section~\ref{sec:scope} and as a limitation in the
concluding chapter: \sysname{} as evaluated requires a
working network path between the service office and the cloud backend.
Second, offline-capable operation --- local inference with deferred
synchronisation --- is recorded as the highest-priority recommendation
for future work, because the survey shows it is the problem the
operators of the current system would most want solved.

\section{\sysname{} System Performance}
\label{sec:sop2-results}
\label{sec:design}

This section addresses Statement of the Problem No.~2: \emph{What is
the level of accuracy of the face-recognition system in identifying
enrolled students and assigning queue numbers without requiring manual
input?} It reports recognition accuracy in two parts that answer
different questions: an offline evaluation on photographs whose
identities are known, which measures accuracy against ground truth, and
the system's own record of live recognitions at the queue-zone camera,
which shows how the same rule behaved in operation and whether queue
numbers were issued without manual input. The algorithms being
evaluated are summarised in Section~\ref{subsec:major-algorithms} and
specified in full in Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}.

\textbf{Operational log and observation record.}\label{cr:oplog} The operational log is
generated automatically by the system and records every detection,
recognition decision with its score and margin, queue assignment,
ticket issuance, prediction output, dashboard action, and associated
timestamps. It supplies the live recognition and issuance results for
Statement of the Problem No.~2. The observation record lists, for each
of the 166 observed cashier transactions, the ticket number, the time
the ticket was issued, the time service started and ended, and the
serving window; it supplies the observed waits used for Statement of
the Problem No.~3. The independent observer's ground-truth log and the
semi-structured interview guide of the original design were not used
(Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}).

\subsection{Evaluation Dataset}
\label{subsec:eval-dataset}

The offline evaluation used photographs of 17 individuals, gathered
from a classmate's face-photo collection and supplemented by
photographs of the researcher, with several photographs per person taken
under varied angle, lighting, and background. Each person's photographs
were divided into an enrolment set and a held-out probe set, using
explicit enrolment and verification labels where the collection
provided them and a 70/30 split by file order otherwise. The enrolment
photographs of each person were averaged into one stored embedding,
exactly as the system builds a registered student's profile; no probe
photograph was used for enrolment.

This produced 45 held-out probes. Each probe was compared with every one
of the 17 stored identities, giving 765 comparisons: 45 genuine pairs
and 720 impostor pairs (Table~\ref{tab:eval-dataset}).

The 17 offline test identities were separate from the nine students
enrolled in the live trial. Their embeddings were computed by the
evaluation scripts for this test only and were never loaded into the
live system's roster, and the study reports only aggregate metrics and
non-identifying evaluation counts.

restored person who exposed the need for a stricter margin.

\subsection{Model Selection and Threshold Calibration}
\label{subsec:calibration}

Unlike a classification study, this system does not train a neural
network. Face embeddings are produced by the pretrained InsightFace
\texttt{buffalo\_s} model, whose recognition network was trained by its
developers on a large public face corpus with the ArcFace objective
described in Chapter~1. Training or fine-tuning such a model requires
millions of photographs across tens of thousands of identities, a scale
no single-office deployment can supply; a network fine-tuned on a few
hundred photographs would generalise worse, not better. Using a
pretrained embedding model is the standard engineering choice at this
scale, and it is not the contribution of this study.

The same holds for person detection. YOLOv8n runs with its stock
weights, pretrained on the COCO dataset, in which the person class is
among the most heavily represented. Fine-tuning it on a small custom set
of queue-area images was attempted during development; person detection
became less reliable afterwards, and the change was reverted. The stock
model is therefore the one evaluated here.

\textbf{Model selection.} \texttt{buffalo\_s} is the smaller of
InsightFace's two general-purpose model packs. It was selected because
the deployed backend runs in 512~MB of memory; loading only its
detection and recognition modules, rather than the full pack, reduced
its footprint from 251.9~MB to 98.9~MB and allowed the service to run
within that limit.

\textbf{What was calibrated.} The contribution of this study is the
decision rule applied on top of the model
(Equation~\ref{eq:face-decision}), and what had to be established
empirically are its two thresholds: the minimum similarity
$\tau_{\mathrm{match}}$ and the required lead over the runner-up
$\tau_{\mathrm{margin}}$. These cannot be derived from first principles;
they depend on the enrolled population, the camera, and the lighting.

The match threshold was selected by sweeping $\tau_{\mathrm{match}}$
from $0.20$ to $0.70$ and recording, for every probe, whether the rule
linked it correctly, linked it to the wrong identity, or refused it.
Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-sweep} shows the result. No threshold in the
range produced a wrong identity; raising the threshold only converted
correct links into refusals. $\tau_{\mathrm{match}} = 0.30$ was chosen
because it sits below the lowest genuine score observed ($0.321$),
leaving room for live captures, which come from a person crop at
queue-zone distance and score lower than posed photographs.

The margin was first set at $0.10$. A sweep over enrolled probes could
not test the case that matters most --- a person who is not enrolled at
all --- so the open-set test described in
Section~\ref{subsec:heldout-accuracy} was added. It found one
non-enrolled person accepted as an enrolled student with a margin of
$0.110$, clearing $0.10$ by a hundredth. Across the corrected dataset,
genuine probes led their runner-up by at least $0.222$ (excluding the
one probe refused at every setting), while strangers led by at most
$0.110$. The margin was therefore raised to $0.15$, which lies between
the two with room on both sides and costs no genuine match.

\subsection{Evidence for the Margin Requirement}
\label{subsec:margin-evidence}

Because the audit table stores both the accepted score and its margin,
the runner-up similarity of every accepted recognition can be recovered
as $s^{(2)} = s^{(1)} - \mathrm{margin}$. This makes it possible to ask
how often the second-best candidate was itself above the acceptance
threshold, which is precisely the case an absolute threshold cannot
resolve.

The highest runner-up so far occurred on the face-login path, where the
system scored $0.7884$ against the correct student with a margin of
$0.4733$, placing the runner-up at $0.3150$. Two further evaluations on
that path produced runner-ups of $0.3096$ and $0.2997$. All three are at
or above $\tau_{\mathrm{match}} = 0.30$. Under a score-only rule, two
enrolled students would both have qualified for the same face, and the
system would have had to choose between them on a difference it had not
established as meaningful. The margin requirement resolved each case
without ambiguity.

That these occurred on the login path rather than at the camera does not
limit the finding. Both paths run the same matcher against the same
enrolled gallery, so how close two enrolled students can come is a
property of the roster, not of which camera is looking. The highest
runner-up recorded at the queue zone itself is $0.2830$, and the closest
pair of stored enrolment embeddings reaches $0.3561$
(Section~\ref{subsec:enrol-separation}), so the condition is reachable
there and has simply not yet been observed in the smaller number of
queue-zone evaluations.

The runner-up similarity also rises as the enrolled population grows. In
recognitions performed while two to four students were enrolled, the
runner-up ranged from $0.05$ to $0.14$; with eight or nine enrolled it
ranged from $0.23$ to $0.31$. This is the expected consequence of adding
identities to an open roster: each additional enrolment is another chance
that some enrolled face resembles the person at the camera. It also
indicates that an absolute threshold alone becomes progressively less
sufficient at the population sizes a campus deployment would reach, which
is the practical argument for the margin requirement rather than a higher
fixed threshold.

\subsection{Issuing the Queue Number Without Manual Input}
\label{subsec:sop2-issuance}

The second half of Statement of the Problem No.~2 concerns what follows a
successful identification: whether the queue number is issued without
manual input, and how quickly. Each issuance is recorded separately from
the recognition audit, with how the number was linked and the timestamps
around it.

Seven numbers were issued over the testing period, before the
supervised trial. All seven were linked
by \texttt{face\_only}, the path in which the system mints the number
itself on an accepted match; none required a staff member to type a

produced these seven issuances, the face-extraction stage returned a
usable embedding on 103 of 103 attempts.

Timing is reported in two consecutive phases, shown in

\subsection{Assessment of Statement of the Problem No.~2}
\label{subsec:sop2-scope}

Statement of the Problem No.~2 asks what level of accuracy the
face-recognition system reaches in identifying enrolled students and
assigning queue numbers without manual input. On held-out photographs
with known identities, the system linked $97.8\%$ of enrolled students
automatically, ranked the correct student first in every case, assigned
no one to a wrong identity, and refused every one of 45 non-enrolled
probes. In live operation at the queue-zone camera, every queue number
issued --- seven of seven --- was minted by the system without staff or
student input, with a mean of $4.80$~s from the moment the system could
act to the number being issued. The rule's defining property held in
both settings: when it could not identify someone with confidence, it
refused rather than guessed.

These findings must be interpreted with four limitations.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The offline figures are an upper bound.} The held-out
        probes are posed, close-up photographs. Live captures are person
        crops at queue-zone distance and score lower, so live accuracy
        should be expected to fall below the offline figures, most
        likely as more refusals rather than as wrong identities.
  \item \textbf{Live recognitions have no ground truth.} The live
        results in Sections~\ref{subsec:enrol-separation}
        to~\ref{subsec:sop2-issuance} come from the system's own record.
        They have not been checked against an independent observer's
        log, so the proportion of correct assignments over total
        arrivals and the re-identification reliability defined in
        Equation~\ref{eq:accuracy} remain to be measured during the
        evaluation period, together with the ticket and token validation
        rate and the detection-to-ticket latency.
  \item \textbf{The populations are small.} The offline evaluation
        covers 17 identities and the deployment nine enrolled students.
        Each additional enrolment adds another face that could resemble
        a new arrival, so the separation figures should be recomputed as
        enrolment grows.
  \item \textbf{No liveness check.} The system does not test whether
        the face in front of the camera belongs to a live person, so it
        cannot distinguish a student from a printed or on-screen
        photograph of that student.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Wait-Time Prediction Accuracy}
\label{sec:sop3-results}

This section addresses Statement of the Problem No.~3: \emph{What is
the level of accuracy of the system's wait-time prediction model in
terms of Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error
(MAPE)?}

\subsection{Wait-Time Predictor Training Result}
\label{sec:wait-time-training-result}
After cleaning and wait-time reconstruction, the prepared dataset
contained 30,362 supervised rows. Applying the one-minute minimum-wait
filter left 19,119 modeling rows. The date-based split produced 13,031
training rows and 6,088 test rows. The training period covered
May~24,~2023 to January~20,~2026, and the test period covered
January~21,~2026 to May~4,~2026. On the test set, the Gradient
Boosting model obtained MAE~=~2.3512 minutes, RMSE~=~4.0858 minutes,
MAPE~=~80.74\%, and $R^2=-0.0349$. The MAE and RMSE are used as the
main accuracy indicators, while MAPE is reported with caution because
short reconstructed waits inflate percentage error. The negative $R^2$
means that, on the test dates, the model's squared error was slightly
larger than that of predicting the test-period average for every
record. Although the model's inputs included an estimate of the number
of people ahead, the log records neither when each student joined the
queue nor when each was called, so both that estimate and the
reconstructed wait are approximate. The following subsection examines
the relationship on transactions for which both times were observed.

\subsection{Evaluation on Observed Cashier Transactions}
\label{subsec:sop3-offline}

\textbf{Data.} The 166 manually observed transactions
(Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}) span three service days: 26 on
8~September, 64 on 9~September, and 76 on 22~September~2026. The
observed wait ranged from 0 to 12 minutes, with a mean of 3.16, a
median of 2.0, and a standard deviation of 2.67 minutes; 18
transactions (10.8\%) had no wait. One issuance time recorded as 00:30
lies outside the office's hours and between tickets issued at 12:25 and
12:36; it was corrected to 12:30 as a 12-hour-clock entry error. The
calibrated predictor's MAE is between 1.85 and 1.88 minutes whether the
record is corrected, removed, or kept.

\textbf{People ahead at issuance.} Windows~3, 4, and~5 call from one
shared ticket sequence, and the observations confirm that service
followed it: fewer than 1\% of pairs of tickets on each day were called
out of ticket-number order. The number of people ahead of a student can
therefore be computed from what was known at the moment the ticket was
issued, as the student's ticket number minus the highest ticket number
already called, minus one. It could be computed for 162 of the 166
transactions; the other four were issued before any observed ticket had
been called that day. Because tickets that were issued but never served
at these windows are included, this count is an overestimate, and it is
reported as a reconstruction rather than a measured queue length.
Figure~\ref{fig:sop3-ahead} shows that waiting time rises with it
(Spearman $\rho = 0.31$), from a median of 2 minutes with no one ahead
to 6 minutes with six to ten people ahead.

for each day, and the errors on the three unseen days were pooled.
Holding out whole days prevents a predictor from learning a day's
congestion from neighbouring transactions of the same day, which a
random split of the 166 records would allow; under a random split the
time-of-day Random Forest appears to beat the baseline (MAE 1.93
against 2.04 minutes), but on unseen days it does not.

the day's first observed ticket when none was observed in that
window), the service time taken from the last ten completed
transactions (or the training days' average when fewer than three had
been completed), and $c = 3$; the
simpler capacity rule \emph{people ahead $\times$ service time $\div$
counters}; and a calibrated model, a median regression of waiting time
on the number of people ahead, capped at a value selected using the
two training days only. The serving window and the student's own

window becomes free and the service time is known only after the
transaction ends.

The two uncalibrated rules over-predicted. The capacity rule, which
assumes that every person ahead occupies a counter for a full average
service time, overestimated because the reconstructed count includes
tickets that were never served. The current-wait formula overestimated
far more, predicting an average of 6.7 minutes against an observed
3.2, and over-predicting 75\% of the transactions. Under light load
($\rho < 1$) the formula's term $Q/\lambda$ grows as arrivals slow down,
whereas the observed waits grew by about 0.3 to 0.4 minutes per person
ahead whether arrivals were slow, moderate, or fast.

\subsection{Assessment of Statement of the Problem No.~3}
\label{subsec:sop3-assessment}

Statement of the Problem No.~3 asks for the level of accuracy of the
system's wait-time prediction model in terms of MAE and MAPE. Two kinds
of predictor were trained and evaluated offline. Evaluated on the
held-out dates of the historical cashiering log, the Gradient Boosting
predictor has an MAE of 2.35 minutes and a MAPE of 80.74\%: its estimate
differs from the reconstructed wait by about two and a half minutes on
average, and the percentage error is high because most waits are short.
This predictor was not deployed (Section~\ref{sec:ml-training-protocol}).

The evaluation on the 166 observed transactions qualifies and extends
this answer. On those transactions, a predictor based on the number of
people ahead reached an MAE of 1.85 minutes and a MAPE of 57.7\% (WAPE
58.4\%) on unseen days, lower than the 2.07 minutes of a constant-median
baseline, while a predictor using only the time of day did not improve
on the baseline. It shows the accuracy that is attainable when the
estimate is driven by the live state of the queue rather than by the
calendar, and the number of people ahead is information that \sysname{}
knows in real time. A predictor of this form, refitted on October 2026
transactions, is the deployed system's benchmark and fallback; the
default estimate simulates the line window by window (Algorithm~4).
That simulation has not yet been measured against real waits: every
number issued now records its estimate and, when called, its real wait,
so its live MAE and MAPE will come from the system's own log once
numbers are issued in regular use. The results also show that the analytical formula must be
calibrated against observed waits before its output is shown to
students. Three limitations qualify these findings: the evaluation covers three service
days and 166 transactions; the number of people ahead was reconstructed
from ticket numbers rather than counted; and the live, per-horizon
comparison of the deployed system's estimates with stopwatch waits was
not conducted and remains to be carried out.

\section{User Satisfaction}
\label{sec:sop4-results}

This section addresses Statement of the Problem No.~4: \emph{What is
the level of user's satisfaction in terms of system's usability,
system's reliability, and system clarity?} Results are reported per
dimension and per respondent group using the weighted mean and overall
weighted mean, interpreted against the Likert scale in
Table~\ref{tab:likert}. The responses come from the shortened evaluation
protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:datagathering}, and describe
users' first experience of the system rather than satisfaction after
sustained use.

Twenty-eight respondents answered: twenty students and eight cashier
staff. The students answered from 25 to 28 September 2026, after the
supervised trial. The cashier staff answered on 27 and 28 September
2026, after being given the staff dashboard to use. Because the system
was not deployed at the cashiering office, the staff did not run it with
a live queue at the counter; their ratings describe a short hands-on
session with the dashboard, and items such as R3-f and R5-f, which
concern the queue matching the students actually present, could be
judged only from what that session showed.

\subsection{Profile of the Respondents}
\label{subsec:sop4-profile}

All twenty-eight respondents confirmed that they were 18 or older and gave
their consent before answering. Of the twenty students, sixteen
($80.0\%$) were in fourth year, two ($10.0\%$) in third year, one
($5.0\%$) in first year, and one ($5.0\%$) in fifth year or above. Asked
how they received their queue number during the session, fifteen students
($75.0\%$) answered that it was issued automatically after the camera

\subsection{Reliability of the Questionnaire}
\label{subsec:sop4-reliability}

twenty-eight respondents answered them and are analysed as one group,

each form describe role-specific tasks and are not used for the
coefficient. Because the questionnaire was not
content-validated by expert raters, alpha is the only reported check on
the instrument's measurement properties. Every common-item scale is
above the $0.70$ threshold.

\subsection{Number Issuance and Recognition as Reported}
\label{subsec:uat-issuance}

The answers to the three questions on the number and on recognition
cannot be taken at face value. Thirty students ($71.4\%$) answered that
their number was issued automatically after the camera recognised them,
and one ($2.4\%$) that staff issued it, although the detector was not
run and the log shows no number issued. Since the
face sign-in in the app also recognises the student by camera, these
students most likely took that recognition for the one at the queue
area, or answered from the diorama presentation. The eleven ($26.2\%$)
who answered that they received no number described the session
accurately. Fifteen students ($35.7\%$) reported at least one
recognition failure, in line with the thirteen refused face sign-ins in
the log. The three ($7.1\%$) who answered that a number went to the
wrong person cannot have meant a queue number, since none was issued;
two of them also rated R2-s \emph{Strongly Agree}. These questions need
to name the step they ask about.

\subsection{Comfort with Face Identification and Open-Ended Responses}
\label{subsec:uat-open}

Asked how comfortable they were with the system using their face to
identify them, four students ($9.5\%$) answered very comfortable, twelve
($28.6\%$) comfortable, nineteen ($45.2\%$) neutral, and seven
($16.7\%$) uncomfortable. This is less favourable than in the September
trial, in which fourteen of the twenty students ($70.0\%$) were
comfortable or very comfortable and one was uncomfortable.

Asked what they liked most, students most often named joining and
following the queue from the phone instead of lining up at the cashier
--- one wrote, ``Tracking my queue number even when I'm not at the
Cashier Line'' --- followed by ease of use and the interface, and
signing in by face. Asked what they would change, five students named
face recognition: it was slow, did not always work, or they would
prefer an ID, a PIN, or a fingerprint. One wrote that it ``is extremely
slow. Even after following the instructions and doing everything
required, it still takes too long to process and sometimes does not
work at all.'' Six asked for changes to the mobile app's interface: the
tab-switching animation, the placement of \emph{Get a Queue Number} and
\emph{Sign out}, clearer colours, and a dark mode. One reported a button
that did not respond, and one an error that needed a restart. Single
students asked for a notification before their turn, payment within the
app, and an automatic registrar slip. One student questioned the
benefit itself: ``you still have to log in and wait for your turn, just
like at the registrar \ldots{} If the goal is to save students time,
then the system should actually make the process faster, not just show
them how long they have to wait.''

One student named as a strength that the system ``does not store your
sensitive information such as facial recognition on the database''.
\sysname{} does store a face embedding for every registered student
(Section~\ref{sec:ethics}). The consent screen says that the photos are
discarded but that a ``face signature'' computed from them is kept; this
student appears to have taken in only the first half, even though C5-s,
on understanding what the system keeps, was rated $4.14$.

Taken together, the UAT shows that students accept the mobile
application's steps of the completed system --- signing in, registering
a face, and joining the queue --- and that these steps depend on the
internet connection: on a poor connection, three of the eighteen face
registrations were lost and three more took five minutes or longer. It
does not show whether students accept the camera step, the number, or
being called, because those steps were shown on the diorama but not
run; students last used them
in the September trial (Section~\ref{sec:sop4-results}), before the
changes for the final defense. This supports two recommendations
(Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}): offline-capable operation, which
the staff's chief concern in the baseline survey already called for, and
repeating the UAT with the queue-zone camera running.

\chapter{Summary, Conclusions, and Recommendations}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This chapter summarizes the findings for each Statement of the Problem,
states the conclusions drawn from them together with the limitations of
the evaluation, and presents the recommendations that follow.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary-findings}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Current problems with the manual queue process
        (Statement of the Problem No.~1).} The baseline survey of 90
        respondents, 83 students and 7 staff, found that students
        strongly agreed that the current process leaves them without
        information, with an overall weighted mean of $4.64$ (Strongly
        Agree). Their highest-rated problems were that the display shows
        only the number being served ($4.86$), that they must keep
        watching it to know when their number is called ($4.80$), and
        that waiting feels unpredictable ($4.78$); $96.4\%$ said the
        current system gives no estimated wait.
        Staff returned an overall weighted mean of $3.34$ (Neutral): they
        agreed that the display does not show the wait ($4.14$) and that
        waits increase at peak periods ($4.29$), but did not find the
        process difficult to run. Asked to name the biggest problem in
        their own words, six of the seven staff named the internet
        connection.

  \item \textbf{Face-recognition accuracy and queue-number issuance
        (Statement of the Problem No.~2).} On 45 held-out photographs of
        17 identities, the threshold-and-margin rule linked 44 ($97.8\%$)
        to the correct student, linked none to a wrong identity, and
        refused one; the correct student was ranked first in every case.
        In the open-set test, all 45 non-enrolled probes were refused,
        including five that an absolute threshold alone would have
        accepted as enrolled students. At the queue-zone camera, the 44
        logged evaluations separated into 18 accepted and 26 refused with
        no overlap in score or margin. All seven queue numbers issued
        during system testing, before the supervised trial, were minted
        by the system without manual input,
        with a mean of $4.80$~s from the moment the system could act to
        the number being issued. Under a load of 50 concurrent users, the
        backend answered 2,536 requests with no failures, 95\% of them
        within 31~ms.

  \item \textbf{Wait-time prediction accuracy (Statement of the Problem
        No.~3).} Evaluated offline on the held-out dates of the
        historical cashiering log, the Gradient Boosting predictor reached
        an MAE of $2.35$ minutes, an RMSE of $4.09$ minutes, a MAPE of
        $80.74\%$, and an $R^2$ of $-0.035$. On 166 manually observed
        transactions evaluated on unseen days, a predictor calibrated on
        the number of people ahead reached an MAE of $1.85$ minutes and a
        MAPE of $57.7\%$, lower than the $2.07$ minutes of a constant-median
        baseline on each of the three days (95\% interval for the
        reduction: 0.04 to 0.40 minutes). A predictor using only the time
        of day did not improve on the baseline, and the analytical
        current-wait formula over-predicted, with an MAE of $4.10$
        minutes. The Gradient Boosting predictor was therefore not
        deployed; the system estimates the wait by simulating the line,
        with the people-ahead regression as its fallback, and the
        simulation's live accuracy is still to be measured.

  \item \textbf{User satisfaction (Statement of the Problem No.~4).}
        Twenty students who used \sysname{} in the supervised trial
        rated it \emph{Strongly Agree} (Excellent) on all three
        dimensions, with an overall weighted mean of $4.32$: usability
        $4.34$, reliability $4.31$, and clarity $4.31$. Their
        highest-rated item was the convenience of asking to be queued
        from the mobile app ($4.45$). Three students reported one
        recognition failure, and four said staff issued their number
        because recognition failed; one student appears in both counts.
        Eight cashier staff, after a short hands-on session with the staff
        dashboard, rated it \emph{Agree} (Very Satisfactory), with an
        overall weighted mean of $3.86$: clarity $4.28$, usability $3.75$,
        and reliability $3.55$. Their only Neutral item was that they did
        not encounter errors that stopped them ($3.00$), and one reported
        the system going offline during brownouts. Students rated five of
        the six common items higher than the staff did.

  \item \textbf{User acceptance test.} On 16~November~2026, after a
        diorama presentation, forty-two students used or watched the
        mobile application on a poor laboratory connection; the
        queue-zone detector was not run, so no number was issued. They
        rated the steps they performed or watched --- signing in,
        registering a face, and joining the queue --- \emph{Agree} (Very
        Satisfactory), with a weighted mean of $4.13$ ($\alpha = 0.857$);
        signing in with Gbox was rated highest ($4.38$). The system's log
        shows fifteen of eighteen face registrations saved and 26 of 39
        face sign-ins accepted. Seven students were uncomfortable with
        being identified by face, and nineteen were neutral.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\textbf{Statement of the Problem No.~1.} The current process does not
fail at issuing numbers; it fails at informing the people who wait. The
cost of that gap falls on students, who cannot see their position or
their remaining wait, while staff, who can see the line from the
counter, do not experience it as a problem. The features that answer the
students' highest-rated problems are therefore the queue position, the
estimated wait, and a notification before the turn arrives. The
operators' own chief concern, the reliability of the internet
connection, is not solved by \sysname{}.

\textbf{Statement of the Problem No.~2.} On the calibration data, the
face-recognition rule identified enrolled students accurately and
refused every non-enrolled person, and the margin requirement, the
element this study contributes, is what prevented the false
identifications that an absolute threshold alone would have produced.
\sysname{} can issue a queue number to a recognized student without
manual input. These results establish that the rule works as designed;
they do not yet establish its accuracy in regular operation, because the
live evaluations have not been checked against an independent
observer's log.

\textbf{Statement of the Problem No.~3.} In offline testing, the
system's wait-time predictor estimates the wait within about two and a
half minutes on average, but no better than the typical wait itself, as
its negative $R^2$ shows. The observed transactions show that estimates
become more accurate when they are driven by the number of people ahead,
which supports the design decision to measure the queue live rather than
predict from the calendar. They also show that the analytical formula
must be calibrated against observed waits before its estimates are shown
to students.

\textbf{Statement of the Problem No.~4.} On first experience, both
groups are satisfied with \sysname{}, the students highly so. Students
rate mobile queue joining most highly and agree that the system shows
their position and estimated wait clearly. Some reported recognition
failures or receiving a number from staff, but none reported receiving
someone else's number. The cashier staff find the dashboard clear and
usable but are less sure of its reliability, and their concern is not
wrong identifications but the system stopping, as it does when the
connection or power fails. Usability is the students' highest-rated
dimension, while clarity is the staff's; reliability is the staff's
lowest. Whether this satisfaction holds after
sustained daily use at the cashiering office cannot be concluded from
the present data.

\textbf{User acceptance test.} Students accept the mobile application's
steps of the completed system: signing in, registering a face, and
joining the queue. These steps depend on the internet connection; on a
poor connection, three of eighteen face registrations were lost and
three more took five minutes or longer. Whether students accept the
camera step and the steps that follow it after the final-defense changes
cannot be concluded, because the detector was not run during the test.
Identification by face is also accepted less readily than the rest of
the system.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \sysname{} as evaluated requires a working internet connection:
        recognition and queue-number issuance stop when the office loses
        its connection to the cloud backend.
  \item The prototype covers one service office and one camera-defined
        queue zone. Walk-ins continue to use the office's existing kiosk,
        and its numbers run separately from the numbers \sysname{}
        issues.
  \item The live results for Statement of the Problem No.~2 come from the
        system's own record. Queue-assignment accuracy,
        re-identification reliability, the ticket and token validation
        rate, and detection-to-ticket latency were not measured against
        an independent observer.
  \item The offline face-recognition results come from posed photographs
        of 17 identities, on which the thresholds were also calibrated,
        so they are an upper bound on live accuracy. The system does not
        check whether a face belongs to a live person.
  \item The wait-time evaluation is offline. The historical waits are
        reconstructed from the log, the observed data cover three service
        days, the number of people ahead was reconstructed from ticket
        numbers rather than counted, and no result is reported per
        prediction horizon.
  \item The baseline is a self-selected sample: 83 students, after
        excluding 72 invalid or unconfirmed submissions, and only 7 staff.
        The students answered in October and November 2026, after the
        supervised trial, although none of them had used \sysname{} at
        the office.
  \item \sysname{} was not deployed at the cashiering office. The live
        results and the user evaluation come from a test installation on the
        NCF campus
        with volunteer students, not from students transacting at the
        counter during office hours.
  \item The user evaluation reflects twenty volunteer students,
        sixteen of them in fourth year, answering after their first
        supervised use of the system, and eight cashier staff answering
        after a short hands-on session with the staff dashboard rather
        than after running a live queue at the counter. Eleven of the
        twenty-eight respondents gave the same rating to all six shared
        items, which can raise Cronbach's alpha; excluding those responses
        reduces the six-item coefficient from $0.902$ to $0.808$.
        With eight staff respondents, one person's answers move an item's
        mean by as much as half a point. The questionnaire was not
        content-validated by expert raters, so no Content Validity Index
        supports its items.
  \item The user acceptance test is one session with forty-two
        students, forty of them in second year, and no cashier staff, in
        a campus laboratory rather than at the cashiering office, on an
        internet connection that the researchers observed to be poor.
        The queue-zone detector was not run, so no number was issued and
        the camera step was shown only on a diorama; some respondents
        only watched the app being used, and the questionnaire cannot
        separate them from those who used it. Its questionnaire differs
        from the September one and, like it, was not content-validated,
        and nine students gave the same rating to every statement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Offline-capable operation} is the highest-priority
        recommendation: local inference at the office with deferred
        synchronization to the cloud, so that recognition and number
        issuance continue when the connection fails, which is the problem
        six of the seven staff named, together with a backup power supply
        for the detector machine and network equipment, since a staff
        member in the evaluation reported the system going offline during
        brownouts, and a poor connection during the user acceptance test
        caused errors and delays while students signed in and registered
        their faces.
  \item \textbf{Evaluate recognition in regular operation} against an
        independent observer's log, reporting queue-assignment accuracy,
        re-identification reliability, the ticket and token validation
        rate, and detection-to-ticket latency.
  \item \textbf{Test the decision thresholds on a larger, independent set}
        of enrolled students and strangers, and recompute the separation
        between enrolled students as enrolment grows.
  \item \textbf{Add a liveness check} before an accepted face can produce
        a queue number, so that a photograph of a student cannot be used
        in their place.
  \item \textbf{Calibrate the current-wait estimate against observed
        waits} and base it on the number of people ahead and the counters
        open, which the observed transactions showed to be the
        information that predicts the wait.
  \item \textbf{Evaluate prediction accuracy live}, comparing the
        system's estimates at each horizon with stopwatch waits over
        several weeks that include every weekday and at least one peak
        period, and record each student's queue position when the number
        is issued.
  \item \textbf{Retrain a learned wait-time model on the system's own
        log.} Every issued number now records its estimate, the people
        ahead, the windows open, and its real wait. Once this log covers
        several weeks, including peak periods, a learned model such as
        the Gradient Boosting predictor can be retrained on exact
        counts and deployed only if it is more accurate than the line
        simulation on unseen days.
  \item \textbf{Issue the number from the application and make the camera
        passive}, as the school's Technology Resources Center suggested:
        a student who asks for a number receives it at once, and the
        camera is used to count the people in the area, mark whether a
        called student has arrived, and feed the wait estimate, instead
        of gating the number. This removes the line at the camera but
        changes the role of computer vision, so it should be evaluated
        as a separate design.
  \item \textbf{Future researchers} may extend the prototype to several
        queue zones and offices and to priority queues, which the present
        scope excludes.
  \item \textbf{Deploy \sysname{} at the cashiering office and repeat the
        user evaluation after sustained operation there}, with the
        cashier staff operating the dashboard, a larger student sample,
        and the
        questionnaire content-validated by expert raters beforehand, so
        that satisfaction can be compared between the groups and measured
        beyond first use.
  \item \textbf{Control the lighting at the queue area} and capture
        enrolment photos under varied lighting, since the one recognition
        failure students explained was attributed to background lighting.
  \item \textbf{Keep face recognition optional}, and state in the mobile
        app that a student may sign in with Gbox and take a printed kiosk
        ticket instead, since five of the twenty students were neutral
        about being identified by face, one was uncomfortable, and one
        asked for it to change; in the user acceptance test, seven of
        forty-two were uncomfortable, nineteen neutral, and five asked for
        it to change.
  \item \textbf{Revise the mobile app's navigation} as the UAT students
        asked: open the app on \emph{Get a Queue Number}, move
        \emph{Sign out} off the home screen, replace the tab-switching
        animation, and add a dark mode.
  \item \textbf{Make the stored face signature stand out on the consent
        screen}, ahead of the statement that photos are discarded, since
        one UAT student believed the system stores no face data.
  \item \textbf{Repeat the user acceptance test with the queue-zone
        detector running}, cashier staff at the dashboard, and a reliable
        connection, so that recognition at the queue area, the issuing of
        the number, calling by window, and the estimated against the
        actual wait are tested by the people who use them; record which
        respondents used the app themselves, and word the recognition
        questions so that they name the step, since students took the
        face sign-in for recognition at the queue area.
  \item \textbf{Strengthen the notification before a student's turn},
        which two cashier staff asked for and which students rated as a
        problem of the current process in the baseline survey.
  \item \textbf{Simplify resolving a person the system could not
        identify} from the staff dashboard, the lowest-rated staff
        usability item, since every refusal by the decision rule reaches
        staff through that step.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Baseline Questionnaire on the Current Queue Process}
\label{app:sop1-questionnaire}

These baseline instruments address Statement of the Problem No.~1 and
were administered before deployment. The staff/cashier form is
transcribed below, followed by screenshots of the student Google Form
as presented to respondents. The problem statements use a five-point
agreement scale (5 = Strongly Agree, 1 = Strongly Disagree); agreement
means the stated problem is present. The student form also includes
screening, profile, queueing-practice, open-ended, and optional
face-registration questions.

\section*{Staff / Cashier Form}

\noindent\textbf{Profile.} (1)~How long have you been handling queue
transactions in this office? (2)~On average, how often do you
personally handle queue management during busy periods?

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Perceived problems on wait-time}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The current display only shows the number being served, not how
        long a client still needs to wait.
  \item It is difficult to predict how long a client will wait after
        receiving their paper ticket.
  \item Wait times increase significantly during peak or busy periods.
  \item Wait times increase further when a window is closed or on break,
        since queue numbers are not redistributed to open windows.
  \item It is difficult to manage the queue efficiently when a window
        has to close temporarily during busy periods.
  \item No-show clients disrupt the flow of the numbering system.
  \item There is no way to notify clients in advance that their number
        is about to be called.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Perceived problems on user experience}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Clients frequently approach the counter to ask about their
        number or wait time.
  \item Clients get confused when a window shows no number, since the
        display does not indicate whether it is closed, on break, or not
        yet in use.
  \item It is difficult to manage complaints related to long or unclear
        wait times.
  \item Handling paper tickets creates additional workload for staff
        during busy periods.
  \item It is challenging to maintain order in the queue area during
        busy periods despite having a numbering system.
  \item Miscommunication with clients occurs when the display and the
        actual queue status do not match.
  \item Overall, the current queue system negatively affects the quality
        of service provided to clients.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Open-ended.} In your own words, what is the biggest
problem you experience with the current queue process?

\section*{Questionnaire Notes}

Each respondent answers fifteen items, five in each dimension of
Statement of the Problem No.~4. Two items in every dimension (U1--U2,
R1--R2, C1--C2) are common to both forms, so that students and staff
can be compared on the same statements; the other three are specific to
the respondent's role, since a student cannot judge the staff dashboard
and a staff member cannot judge the mobile application. Every statement
is worded so that agreement is favourable, so no item needs
reverse-scoring.

Weighted means are computed per item, per dimension, and per group as
described in Section~2.7. Under the shortened evaluation protocol, the
questionnaire was not content-validated by expert raters, and its
internal consistency is measured with Cronbach's alpha on the responses
themselves rather than on a separate pilot sample. The questions in Section~IV are reported descriptively and
do not enter any weighted mean.
